# NICE: neural-initialised static condensation with equilibrium correction, with application to the thickness design of cut TPMS lattices

## Abstract

Lattices trimmed to a part's shape have cut cells that can carry supports and loads, each with its own geometry. Modelling every wall makes each design iteration a fine-scale analysis of the structure; homogenisation is inaccurate where cells are not small relative to it. Static condensation of each cell as a superelement keeps its fine scale but needs an interior factorisation for every geometry. NICE avoids it, retaining the degrees of freedom on the cell faces and in the elements cut by the trimming plane. A network taking the cell geometry as input predicts the interior displacements, one fixed two-grid cycle reduces interior force imbalance, and the condensed stiffness follows from the strain energy of the corrected field. The prediction is linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them and rigid-body motions exactly. The condensed stiffness is therefore symmetric positive semidefinite with zero energy only for rigid-body motions, never softer than the exact one, with error quadratic in the interior displacement error. Under stated conditions the correction cannot increase this error. Because accurate compliance does not ensure accurate sensitivities, both are trained and checked. For cut Schwarz-P cells, the mean energy error over 80 validation geometries is 0.074%, and an eight-cell analysis with sensitivities is about ten times faster than direct solution. Thickness optimisation reproduces the exact-condensation design of an eight-cell lattice. For a 24-cell layer clamped at its cut, where homogenisation underestimates the compliance by 27%, NICE agrees with exact condensation to 0.03% in compliance and 0.3% in gradient.

**Keywords:** superelement; static condensation; neural network; multigrid; cut finite element method; TPMS lattice; thickness optimisation.

## 1. Introduction

Graded lattices [Yu et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matdes.2019.108021) and lattices trimmed to the shape of a part consist of many cells of different geometry. Their thickness design faces two competing requirements. The structure is large and must be reanalysed at every design iteration. Yet the quantities that drive the design are local, for example the stiffness of a cut cell next to a support or the sensitivity of the compliance to a wall thickness. Homogenisation replaces the cells by an equivalent material; it is inexpensive and is the usual basis for the design of graded lattices [Li et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2018.06.003), [Wu et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00158-021-02881-8). Its accuracy is limited, however, where the cells are not small compared with the scale on which the field varies, in particular at supports, at loads and in cells cut by the part boundary. For the plate of Section 5.10, a single layer of cells clamped at its cut (Figure 1), the homogenised model underestimates the compliance of the uniform starting design by 27%, whereas the method of this paper underestimates it by 0.011%. A fine-scale analysis of every wall avoids this error and is feasible with large-scale parallel solvers [Aage et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23911), but since each design iteration changes every cell, it must be repeated at every iteration (Section 5.9).

Substructuring lies between these two approaches. Each cell is treated as a substructure, or superelement, and statically condensed onto the retained (master) degrees of freedom through which it connects to its neighbours, supports and loads [Guyan (1965)](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2874), [Irons (1965)](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3027). The assembled system contains only the retained degrees of freedom, and after its solution the interior displacements of each cell are recovered and used to evaluate local design quantities such as the sensitivities. Static condensation is exact, but it requires a factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix for every cell geometry. For cut thin-walled cells, the retained degrees of freedom are those of all nodes on the cell faces and of all nodes of the elements intersected by the trimming plane, hereafter referred to as cut-plane elements. For the 80 validation geometries of this paper, a cell has 2,679 to 45,900 retained degrees of freedom, with a median of about \(2.4\times10^4\). Because every design iteration changes the geometry of every cell, these factorisations must be repeated at every iteration.

Learned component models replace the interior solution by a neural network. Some map nodal displacements directly to nodal forces [Capuano & Rimoli (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2018.10.046). Learned substructures [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041), [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955) predict numerical shape functions with a neural network, compute the substructure stiffness matrix as \(N^TKN\) and assemble the substructures like finite elements. These methods describe the boundary displacement of each substructure by a few degrees of freedom, for example through linear or cubic Bézier interpolation, and the network outputs one shape function for each of them. Port reduction [Eftang & Patera (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4543), [Smetana & Patera (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1137/15m1009603), [Ballani et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2017.09.014) likewise reduces the degrees of freedom shared between components. Such a reduction restricts the displacement patterns that neighbouring substructures can exchange, and the resulting error cannot be removed by a better interior approximation. For cut cells, supports and loads on the cut surface act on all degrees of freedom of the cut-plane elements, so these degrees of freedom are retained as well. The number of retained degrees of freedom is then large and changes with the geometry, so that a network with one output per retained degree of freedom does not apply directly.

This paper proposes NICE (neural-initialised static condensation with equilibrium correction). NICE keeps all degrees of freedom on the cell faces and in the cut-plane elements and approximates only the interior displacements. A neural network that takes the cell geometry as input predicts these displacements; it is nonlinear in the geometry but exactly linear in the retained displacements. Its parameters act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels rather than on individual degrees of freedom, so that one network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves cells with different numbers of retained degrees of freedom. After the prediction, one two-grid cycle of Chebyshev smoothing and coarse-grid correction [Xu (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1137/1034116) is applied with the retained displacements held fixed, which reduces the interior force imbalance. The smoothing reduces the high-frequency part of the interior error, and the coarse-grid correction reduces its smooth part. This cycle is the equilibrium correction in the name of the method. The network and the correction together define a linear map from the retained displacements to the displacements of the whole cell. The condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy of the resulting displacement field, and neither this map nor the condensed stiffness is formed as a matrix.

Because the condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy, it is symmetric positive semidefinite, and only the rigid-body modes have zero energy. By the principle of minimum potential energy, it is never softer than the exact condensed stiffness, and its error is quadratic in the interior displacement error. Exact static condensation is the special case of exact interior displacements. These properties hold for any interior approximation that is linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them and rigid-body motions, and therefore do not depend on how well the network is trained. Because the two-grid correction is a fixed linear operation, the corrected condensed stiffness keeps these properties. Under the conditions on the smoothing interval and the coarse solve stated in Proposition 2, its error does not exceed the error before the correction [Golub & Varga (1961)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01386013), [Xu & Zikatanov (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0894-0347-02-00398-3). The network is trained with the correction applied, and the two complement each other. Under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field, which has no trainable parameters, leaves 5.4 to 265 times the mean energy error of NICE (Section 5.5).

An interior displacement error affects the compliance and the sensitivities differently. In the compliance, the error of each cell is weighted by the fraction of the strain energy that the cell carries. In the thickness sensitivity, it enters through the derivative of the stiffness matrix as a term linear in the interior displacement error, which the energy error bounds only at the order of its square root. A cell that carries a small fraction of the energy can therefore leave the compliance accurate while its sensitivity remains inaccurate. For any approximate static condensation used in design, an accurate compliance does not guarantee an accurate sensitivity. Similar observations have been made for approximate reanalysis and inexact solves in topology optimisation [Amir et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.2536), [Amir et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00158-009-0463-4), [Gogu (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4797). Section 4 derives these relations for any interior approximation that meets the conditions above (Propositions 3 and 4). NICE is therefore trained and checked on compliance and sensitivity separately (Section 5.6).

The main contributions are as follows.

- All degrees of freedom on the faces of a cut cell and in its cut-plane elements are retained, and only the interior displacements are approximated. Supports and loads on the cut surface therefore act on the original degrees of freedom, although these change with the geometry, and can be applied without retraining (Sections 2, 5.7 and 5.10).
- A neural network predicts the interior displacements. By construction, its output is exactly linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them and rigid-body motions. Its size does not depend on the number of retained degrees of freedom, and it forms no matrix. The condensed stiffness computed from its strain energy is symmetric positive semidefinite and never softer than the exact one. Its error is quadratic in the interior displacement error (Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.6).
- A fixed two-grid correction inside the condensation reduces the error of a trained network without retraining. The correction keeps these properties for any discrete model with a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix and a positive definite interior block. Under the conditions of Proposition 2, it does not increase the energy error (Sections 3.3 and 3.4). In every example of Supplementary Table ST07, a larger number of smoothing steps reduced the energy error further (Section 5.5).
- The different routes by which the interior displacement error reaches the compliance and the sensitivities are derived. Both quantities are checked separately, and the thickness designs are verified against exact static condensation and compared with homogenisation (Sections 4, 5.6, 5.8 and 5.10).

Section 1.1 reviews related work. Section 2 describes the finite element model of the cut cells, static condensation, the design problem and the assumptions of the analysis. Section 3 presents NICE: the computation of the condensed stiffness, the network that predicts the interior displacements, the two-grid correction, the training and the application of the condensed stiffness without forming it. Section 4 analyses the effect of the interior displacement error on the compliance and the sensitivities; the proofs are given in the appendices. Section 5 presents numerical examples for cut thin-walled Schwarz-P cells in a stabilised cut finite element model. The examples cover the accuracy on 80 validation geometries and on two-cell assemblies, an ablation that restricts the cell-face displacements, and eight-cell lattices of cells not used in training or checkpoint selection. They also cover the computational cost, compared with a direct solve of the whole lattice and with conventional exact static condensation, and thickness optimisation, checked against exact static condensation and compared with a homogenised model. Section 6 discusses the scope and limitations of the method, and Section 7 concludes.

### 1.1. Related work

Among learned substructures, problem-independent machine learning (PIML) has been studied most systematically. PIML predicts numerical shape functions of a substructure for a few boundary degrees of freedom and imposes constraints for rigid-body motion; its self-consistent form computes the substructure stiffness matrix as \(N^TKN\) [Huang et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2022.101887), [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041). Its variants train without precomputed shape-function data by minimising the potential energy [Huang et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmps.2024.105893), impose symmetry equivariance [Jiang, C. et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compstruct.2026.120865), treat isoparametric substructures [Zhang, L. et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2024.102237), transfer learned substructures to complex three-dimensional design domains [Zhang et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmecsci.2026.112007) and optimise lattices [Xu et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compstruct.2025.119330). One variant joins oversampled numerical basis functions by an overlapping partition of unity and corrects the reconstructed fine-scale field by a few Jacobi-preconditioned conjugate gradient iterations on the global equilibrium equations [Guo et al. (2026b)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22019v1). A few boundary degrees of freedom per substructure keep the assembled model small enough for very large structures, and the resulting boundary model error is controlled by refining the partition, enriching the interpolation or oversampling. NICE lies at the other end of this trade-off: it keeps all retained degrees of freedom of a cell, which avoids this boundary model error but enlarges the assembled model. It learns the interior displacements of each geometry in a family whose discrete space changes with the cut, instead of numerical shape functions for a fixed set of boundary degrees of freedom. Another PIML variant replaces the linear interpolation of the boundary displacement by cubic Bézier interpolation and was compared, in the same study, with a variant that predicts numerical shape functions for every boundary node. For voxel substructures, the latter cost more than it saved and was less accurate on the larger substructures, because its network had to predict many more outputs [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955). In NICE, condensation still removes most unknowns, because the interior of a thin-walled cell is several times larger than its retained part; the cell of Figure 1b retains 9,674 of its 72,631 nodes. The network size of NICE does not grow with the number of retained degrees of freedom, and the two-grid correction reduces the error that the network leaves (Section 5.5).

Closest to NICE are component models that keep a mechanical structure. A condensed stiffness can be computed from the strain energy of an approximate displacement field that is linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them and rigid-body motions. By the principle of minimum potential energy, this condensed stiffness is never softer than the exact one, and its error is quadratic in the interior displacement error (Section 3.1). The same property underlies static condensation itself [Toselli & Widlund (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1007/b137868), multiscale finite elements [Hou & Wu (1997)](https://doi.org/10.1006/jcph.1997.5682), component reduced-basis methods [Huynh et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1051/m2an/2012022) and learned shape functions evaluated as \(N^TKN\) [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041). NICE applies it to network-predicted interior displacements on a set of retained degrees of freedom that changes with the cut. Learned and reduced component models differ in three respects: the exchange between a component and its neighbours, the representation of the interior and the place where the approximation is improved. The learned substructures of [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041) and [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955) reduce what a component exchanges and learn shape functions for the reduced boundary. NICE keeps everything a component exchanges and learns only the response of the interior. It represents the interior by a network that takes the cell geometry as input and improves the approximation within the condensed stiffness that is assembled.

As to what a component exchanges, component reduced-basis methods keep the complete interface in the condensation but represent the interior on a discrete space shared by the parameter family and construct reduced bases for it offline [Huynh et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1051/m2an/2012022). For lattices, such component-wise models have been reused in design optimisation, with error bounds on the displacement, the compliance and its sensitivity [McBane & Choi (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2021.113813). For unfitted discretisations, solutions on varying active sets have been extended to a common background space before reduction [Chasapi et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2023.115997). NICE also keeps the complete set of retained degrees of freedom, but this set itself changes with the cut, and its network therefore takes the discrete geometry of each cell as input, instead of the parameters of a shared discretisation.

The representation of a component varies among lattice methods and learned elements. One lattice method condenses the cells into superelements and interpolates their stiffness in a density parameter by a reduced-order surrogate, which supplies explicit stiffness derivatives for optimisation without scale separation [Wu et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2018.11.003). For lattices built by spline composition, reduced-order cell operators have replaced the cell-wise local solves of inexact finite element tearing and interconnecting, dual–primal (FETI-DP) methods [Hirschler et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.7419). For unfitted two-dimensional lattices, balancing domain decomposition by constraints (BDDC) has been combined with full cell stiffness matrices, whose integrals over trimmed cells are approximated by matrix discrete empirical interpolation [Bonilla Moreno et al. (2027)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.119304). Each of these lattice methods keeps one discretisation across cells: a common lattice pattern, a composed parametric cell and one high-order unfitted element per cell, respectively. For unfitted coarse elements, numerical shape functions have also been conditioned against small cuts [Chen & Li (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2026.104038). Learned elements condense an embedded inner domain into a symmetric positive semidefinite interface stiffness [Parish et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00466-024-02481-5). Convex neural energy elements learn boundary energies from targets computed with the exact condensed stiffness and recover the interior fields by exact lifting [Jiang, H. et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02036v1). NICE learns the displacement recovery itself. A single corrected recovery operator \(F\), built without an interior factorisation and applied without forming a matrix, defines the assembled condensed stiffness, the recovered displacements and the thickness sensitivities.

The place where the approximation is improved also differs. Inexact substructuring methods [Dohrmann (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nla.514), [Li & Widlund (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2006.03.011), [Klawonn & Rheinbach (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1758) improve approximate subdomain and coarse solves inside a preconditioner, and their iteration converges to the exact discrete solution (Section 6.2). In a nonlinear example, convex neural energy elements refine the exact lifting by Newton steps and differentiate through these steps [Jiang, H. et al. (2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02036v1). Component reduced-basis methods bound the error of the component or assembled solution a posteriori [Huynh et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1051/m2an/2012022). NICE provides a computable interior residual instead of a certified bound (Eq. (7)). Its correction acts on the interior displacements of each cell at fixed retained displacements, inside the strain energy that defines the condensed stiffness, and therefore changes the assembled condensed stiffness.

Learning has also been combined with multilevel and domain decomposition solvers. Examples are learned multigrid prolongations [Greenfeld et al. (2019)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/greenfeld19a.html), [Luz et al. (2020)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/luz20a.html), machine-learning-assisted adaptive FETI-DP coarse spaces [Heinlein et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1137/20M1344913) and networks trained through the solver [Um et al. (2020)](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/43e4e6a6f341e00671e123714de019a8-Abstract.html). Hybrid neural solvers combine learned predictions with relaxation, learned inverse actions or coarse spaces [Zhang, E. et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00910-x), [Kopaničáková & Karniadakis (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1137/24m162861x), [Xing et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3811333). In optimisation without scale separation, spectral multiscale coarse bases [Efendiev et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcp.2013.04.045) have served as the coarse space of a two-level preconditioner for the fully resolved problem [Alexandersen & Lazarov (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2015.02.028). Geometry-aware neural operators and graph networks [Li et al. (2023a)](https://www.jmlr.org/papers/v24/23-0064.html), [Li et al. (2023b)](https://doi.org/10.52202/075280-1556), [Pfaff et al. (2021)](https://openreview.net/forum?id=roNqYL0_XP), multigrid-structured operators [He et al. (2024)](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/hash/eb3c8135137c8a60425a0320869ad87e-Abstract-Conference.html), learned Green's functions [Boullé & Townsend (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10208-022-09556-w) and training by energy minimisation [E & Yu (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40304-018-0127-z) learn the solution map of the whole problem. Most neural operators learn maps that are nonlinear in their input and do not obey superposition. The strain energy of the predicted field is then not a quadratic form in the retained displacements, and the properties of the condensed stiffness rest on this quadratic form. The methods above accelerate a global solve or approximate the solution of the whole problem. In NICE, the learned map recovers the displacements of a single cell from its retained displacements, and the strain energy of these displacements defines a condensed stiffness that can be assembled, differentiated and improved.

Static condensation is itself a two-level method. The exact recovery \(E\) maps the retained displacements to the equilibrium displacements of the whole cell. In algebraic multigrid terms, \(E\) is the ideal interpolation from the retained to all degrees of freedom, and \(S=E^TKE\) is the corresponding coarse-grid matrix [Falgout & Vassilevski (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1137/S0036142903429742). NICE replaces \(E\) by a recovery operator \(F\) from the retained to the active degrees of freedom and condenses with the coarse-grid matrix \(\widehat S=F^TKF\) of this interpolation; a network predicts this operator, and the two-grid cycle then smooths it. Smoothed-aggregation multigrid similarly improves a tentative prolongation by smoothing [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511). The operator \(F\) is nonlinear in the cell geometry and linear in the retained displacements. Learned multigrid prolongations [Greenfeld et al. (2019)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v97/greenfeld19a.html), [Luz et al. (2020)](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/luz20a.html) are likewise nonlinear in the operator and linear in the vector they act on, but they serve as part of a solver. The operator \(F\) instead defines a component model: it serves sets of retained degrees of freedom that change with the cut, and its coarse-grid matrix is assembled into the lattice and differentiated in design.

## 2. Cut cells, static condensation and the design problem

The cells are discretised by a stabilised cut finite element method (CutFEM) [Burman et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4823) on a fixed Cartesian background mesh. The walls and the cut are not meshed. They determine which background elements are active, and they enter the stiffness through integrals over the material part of each element. A change of wall thickness or of the cut therefore requires no remeshing, and the thickness sensitivity follows from the derivatives of these integrals (Appendix H). All cells also lie on the same background mesh, on which the network of Section 3.2 defines its fixed grid hierarchy. However, the active elements change with the geometry, and so does the set of retained degrees of freedom. Sections 3 and 4 must account for this change. Their relations use only the symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of the resulting stiffness matrix and the positive definiteness of its interior block.

### 2.1. Geometry and finite element model

This paper considers three-dimensional thin-walled cells based on triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS). Each cell occupies the reference box \(\mathcal B=[0,1]^3\), whose six faces are called the cell faces. The material domain of a cell is defined by a Schwarz-P-type trigonometric level-set function, a spatially varying band parameter and an optional planar cut:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&\phi(x)=\sum_{a=1}^{3}\cos(2\pi x_a),\qquad
\tau(x)=\sum_{c\in\{0,1\}^{3}}N_c^{Q_1}(x)\tau_c,\\
&\Omega(\eta)=\{x\in\mathcal B:|\phi(x)|\le\tau(x),\ \boldsymbol n\cdot x\le b_{\rm cut}\}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{1}
\]

The eight parameters \(\tau_c\) are interpolated by the trilinear functions \(N_c^{Q_1}\). They set the width of the implicit band, and thereby the wall thickness and the material distribution. They are called corner thickness parameters, and the derivatives with respect to them are called thickness sensitivities. When the eight corner values are collected in a vector, such as \(\boldsymbol\tau\) or the sensitivity vector \(\boldsymbol s\), the corners are numbered \(c=1,\ldots,8\). The cut plane \(\boldsymbol n\cdot x=b_{\rm cut}\) has the offset \(b_{\rm cut}\) and the unit normal \(\boldsymbol n\), which points away from the material that remains after the cut. For an uncut cell, the half-space constraint is omitted. The symbol \(\eta\) collects the geometry, material and discretisation parameters.

The displacements are approximated by continuous tensor-product \(Q_2\) functions on a Cartesian background mesh. A background element is active if its intersection with \(\Omega(\eta)\) has positive measure. This is certified by interval enclosures of the level-set and band functions over the element (Appendix A.1). The displacement degrees of freedom (DOFs) of the nodes of the active elements are called the active DOFs. Integration over the material domain gives the elastic stiffness of the walls. Ghost-penalty stabilisation [Burman (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.crma.2010.10.006) adds terms that couple neighbouring active elements. These terms control the effect of small cuts, which are active elements that contain very little material [Burman et al. (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4823). The symmetric matrix \(K\) defines the stabilised discrete energy \(\tfrac12u^TKu\). The reference solution in this work is the equilibrium of this discrete system. All errors are measured against this discrete solution, not against the solution of the continuum problem. Figure 1 shows a cut lattice and one of its cut cells. Table 1 (Section 5.1) lists the settings of the examples, and Appendix A gives the details of integration and assembly.

### 2.2. Retained degrees of freedom and static condensation

For each cell, the retained DOFs form the set \(P\), which has two parts (Appendix A.1). One part contains the DOFs of the cell-face nodes of every material patch of positive area on the cell faces. The other part contains all displacement DOFs of the active elements that carry a cut-surface patch of positive area. These elements form the layer of active elements intersected by the cut plane and are called the cut-plane elements. Some of their nodes lie away from the cut plane, but the basis functions of these nodes contribute to the displacement and the virtual work on that plane. The complete set of retained DOFs is used in both the network prediction and the assembly. It includes the DOFs coupled to neighbouring cells and the DOFs on the exterior boundary.

The remaining active DOFs are the interior DOFs and form the set \(I\). The two sets have \(p=|P|\) and \(i=|I|\) DOFs, and \(n_a=p+i\). The selection matrices \(J_P\) and \(J_I\) extract the two sets from the vector \(u\) of active displacements, and \(q=J_Pu\) is the vector of retained displacements. Boundary loads act through \(q\), and interior body loads are zero. With the DOFs ordered as \((P,I)\), static condensation gives the exact displacement recovery \(E\) and the exact condensed stiffness \(S\), that is, the Schur complement:

\[
\begin{aligned}
&K=\begin{bmatrix}K_{PP}&K_{PI}\\K_{IP}&A\end{bmatrix},\qquad
A=K_{II},\qquad
E=\begin{bmatrix}I_p\\-A^{-1}K_{IP}\end{bmatrix},\\
&S=K_{PP}-K_{PI}A^{-1}K_{IP}=E^TKE.
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

The stiffness matrix \(K\) is symmetric positive semidefinite. Its null space consists of the six rigid-body modes, which form the columns of \(R\in\mathbb R^{n_a\times6}\), and their restriction \(R_P=J_PR\) to the retained DOFs has rank six. Two features of the discrete model ensure these properties (Appendix A.1). The geometry generator accepts only cells whose active elements form a single face-connected set. In addition, material that is connected only through partially filled elements is coupled through shared basis functions and the ghost penalty. The interior block \(A\) is then positive definite, because an interior field with zero energy would be a rigid-body motion that vanishes on the retained DOFs. Prescribing \(q\) therefore removes every interior motion with zero energy. The exact displacement recovery \(u=Eq\) is the unique minimiser of the discrete energy subject to \(J_Pu=q\), and \(Sq\) is the vector of the corresponding nodal forces on the retained DOFs.

### 2.3. Assembly, compliance and the design problem

For substructure \(m\), the Boolean assembly map \(B_m\) extracts \(q_m=B_mU\) from the vector \(U\) of free global retained displacements. Coincident cell-face DOFs, identified by their position on the background mesh, are shared between neighbouring cells. Retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements that do not lie on the cell faces belong only to their own substructure. The thickness field on a shared face depends only on the four corner values of that face, and \(\phi\) is periodic. The material patches of two neighbouring cells therefore coincide on every face that is not intersected by a cut. Where a cut removes material from one side of a face only, the unmatched face DOFs remain DOFs of the cell that carries them. Fixed homogeneous supports are incorporated into \(B_m\). The reference and the approximate assembled systems use the same maps \(B_m\):

\[
\mathbb K=\sum_mB_m^TS_mB_m,\qquad
\widehat{\mathbb K}=\sum_mB_m^T\widehat S_mB_m,
\qquad
\mathbb K U=f_g,\quad\widehat{\mathbb K}\widehat U=f_g.
\tag{3}
\]

Here \(\widehat S_m\) is the approximate condensed stiffness of Section 3, and \(f_g\) is the prescribed force on the free retained DOFs. Because the interior DOF sets of different cells are disjoint, condensing each cell before assembly gives the same matrix as condensing the assembled fine-scale system. The supported reference matrix \(\mathbb K\) is assumed to be positive definite.

The compliance is \(C=f_g^TU\), and its approximation is \(\widehat C=f_g^T\widehat U\), with the relative error \(e_C=|\widehat C/C-1|\). The local accuracy of the condensed stiffness is measured by the relative energy error of Section 3.1. The thickness sensitivities of a cell form the eight-component vector \(\boldsymbol s\) of Section 4.2. With \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}\) computed from the approximate displacements, their relative error in the Euclidean norm is \(e_s=\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}-\boldsymbol s\|_2/\|\boldsymbol s\|_2\).

In design, the corner thickness parameters at the lattice vertices are the design variables. They are collected in \(\boldsymbol\tau_g\) and are mapped to the corners of each cell by \(\boldsymbol\tau_m=\boldsymbol\tau_m(\boldsymbol\tau_g)\). The design problem is

\[
\min_{\boldsymbol\tau_g}\;C(\boldsymbol\tau_g)\quad\text{subject to}\quad V(\boldsymbol\tau_g)\le V^*,\qquad \tau_{\min}\le\tau\le\tau_{\max},
\]

where \(V\) is the material volume of the discrete model. In addition, the corner span and the gradient norm of each cell are bounded to keep every cell within the range of geometries used in training (Section 5.10). The compliance gradient collects the cell sensitivities at each vertex, \(\partial C/\partial\boldsymbol\tau_g=\sum_m(\partial\boldsymbol\tau_m/\partial\boldsymbol\tau_g)^T\boldsymbol s_m\), where \(\boldsymbol s_m\) is the eight-component sensitivity vector of cell \(m\) (Section 4.2).

An approximate condensed stiffness \(\widehat S_m\) that replaces \(S_m\) in this design problem must meet three requirements. The matrix \(\widehat S_m\) must be symmetric positive semidefinite, and its null space must consist only of the rigid-body modes of the retained DOFs, the columns of \(R_P\). The supported assembled matrix \(\widehat{\mathbb K}\) of Eq. (3) must be positive definite; this follows from \(\widehat S_m\succeq S_m\) and \(\mathbb K\succ0\) (Appendix B.2). The error of \(\widehat S_m\) must be traceable to the compliance error \(e_C\) and to the sensitivity error \(e_s\), which weight this error differently. Section 3 constructs a condensed stiffness that meets these requirements and needs no interior factorisation for a new geometry, even when the cut changes the discrete space. This condensed stiffness can also be improved at deployment without retraining. Sections 3.1 and 4 derive what any recovery operator that satisfies the assumptions of Section 2.4 provides towards these requirements.

### 2.4. Assumptions

Sections 3 and 4 consider an approximate displacement recovery operator \(F\), which maps the retained displacements \(q\) of a cell to the displacements \(Fq\) of all its active DOFs. Propositions 1, 3 and 4 are stated under the following assumptions, which Appendix B.1 states in full. Proposition 2 also uses (A1) and (A2).

- (A1) The cell stiffness matrix \(K\) is symmetric positive semidefinite, its null space consists of the rigid-body modes, \(A=K_{II}\succ0\), and interior body loads vanish (Section 2.2).
- (A2) The recovery operator \(F\) is linear and reproduces the retained displacements, \(J_PF=I_p\). It also reproduces rigid-body motions, \(FR_P=R\).
- (A3) Cells share only retained DOFs, and the supported assembled stiffness matrix \(\mathbb K\) is positive definite (Section 2.3).
- (D) On a design interval, the discrete choices listed in Appendix B.1 are fixed. They include the active elements, the ghost-penalty faces, the retained and interior DOF sets, the assembly maps with the supports, and the load.

## 3. The NICE method

NICE is defined by one operator, the corrected recovery operator \(F\), which maps the retained displacements of a cell to the displacements of the whole cell. The condensed stiffness \(\widehat S=F^TKF\) follows from the strain energy of the recovered displacement field, and exact static condensation is the case \(F=E\). Section 3.1 computes the condensed stiffness from the strain energy and applies it with the transpose of \(F\). For any recovery operator that reproduces the retained displacements and rigid-body motions, the condensed stiffness is then symmetric positive semidefinite and never softer than the exact one. The interior displacements are predicted by a network that takes the cell geometry as input; the network recovery is exactly linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them by construction (Section 3.2). A fixed two-grid correction \(\mathcal W\) is applied to the predicted displacements before the strain energy is computed, and reduces the interior force imbalance that the network leaves (Sections 3.3 and 3.4); the network is trained with this correction applied (Section 3.5). Section 3.6 describes how the condensed stiffness is applied. Figure 2 shows how these parts define both the action of the condensed stiffness and the displacements recovered after assembly.

### 3.1. Condensed stiffness from the strain energy

The condensed stiffness is defined through the strain energy, under the original stiffness \(K\), of a recovered displacement field. Let \(F\) be a linear recovery operator that maps the retained displacements \(q\) to the displacements \(Fq\) of the whole cell. The operator \(F\) must reproduce the retained displacements, \(J_PF=I_p\), and the rigid-body motions, \(FR_P=R\), where \(R\) contains the rigid-body modes of Section 2.2. Together with linearity, these conditions form assumption (A2) of Section 2.4. In NICE, \(F\) consists of the network recovery operator \(\widehat E\) followed by the two-grid correction \(\mathcal W\), both described at the end of this section. The recovery operator and its condensed stiffness are

\[
F=\mathcal W\widehat E,\qquad
\widehat S=F^TKF,\qquad
\widehat{\mathcal U}(q)=\tfrac12q^T\widehat S q,\qquad
\widehat f_P=\widehat S q.
\tag{4}
\]

Here \(\widehat{\mathcal U}(q)\) is the strain energy of the recovered field, and \(\widehat f_P\) are the corresponding nodal forces on the retained DOFs. The definitions of \(\widehat S\), \(\widehat{\mathcal U}\) and \(\widehat f_P\) in Eq. (4) apply to any recovery operator \(F\). Only the relation \(F=\mathcal W\widehat E\) is specific to NICE. The form \(\widehat S=F^TKF\) coincides with that of substructure methods in which the condensed stiffness matrix is computed from a matrix of numerical shape functions \(N\) as \(N^TKN\) [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041); NICE, however, does not form \(F\). For a virtual retained displacement \(\delta q\), the virtual displacement of the whole cell is \(F\delta q\), and the variation of the strain energy is \(\delta\widehat{\mathcal U}=(F\delta q)^TKFq=\delta q^TF^TKFq\). Applying the condensed stiffness therefore requires the transpose \(F^T\) after the stiffness action. In NICE, this transpose includes every step of \(F\), namely the rigid-body reconstruction, the restoration of the retained displacements and the correction. With \(F_I=J_IF\), the action of the condensed stiffness has the block form

\[
\widehat S q=(KFq)_P+F_I^T(KFq)_I.
\tag{5}
\]

The second term transfers the work of the interior residual \((KFq)_I\), the interior force imbalance of the recovered field, to the retained DOFs. It vanishes when the recovered field is in interior equilibrium and is otherwise needed for the returned forces to be the derivative of the strain energy \(\widehat{\mathcal U}\). The symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of \(\widehat S\) follow from \(K=K^T\succeq0\) and do not require the gather, scatter and grid-transfer maps of the network to be symmetric (Section 3.2). Since the null space of \(K\) consists of the rigid-body modes (Section 2.2) and \(FR_P=R\), the only zero-energy modes of \(\widehat S\) are the retained rigid-body modes (Appendix B). Appendix E derives the transpose \(F^T\). Appendix B.2 shows that, with this transpose, the error of the condensed stiffness is quadratic in the interior error.

Under assumption (A1), the exact field \(Eq\) has the smallest strain energy among all displacement fields with retained displacements \(q\), by the principle of minimum potential energy. Any recovery operator \(F\) with \(J_PF=J_PE=I_p\) differs from \(E\) only in the interior DOFs. Let \(H=J_I(F-E)\) denote this interior error. Because the exact field is in interior equilibrium, \(J_IKE=0\), the cross terms between \(E\) and \(H\) vanish from \(\widehat S=F^TKF\) (Appendix B.2).

**Proposition 1 (Ritz identity).** Under (A1) and (A2), the condensed stiffness of \(F\) exceeds the exact condensed stiffness \(S\) by the energy of the interior error:

\[
\boxed{\widehat S-S=H^TAH\succeq0.}
\tag{6}
\]

An approximate recovery operator therefore adds stiffness in proportion to the energy of its interior error. The error has no first-order term, because the exact field is in interior equilibrium. Exact static condensation is the case \(F=E\), \(H=0\). For a given retained displacement \(q\), called a test displacement, the accuracy of \(\widehat S\) is measured by the relative energy error \(\varepsilon(q)=q^T(\widehat S-S)q/(q^TSq)\), which is nonnegative by Eq. (6). Let \(u=Eq\), \(\widehat u=Fq\) and \(d_I=Hq\). The interior residual \(r_I=(K\widehat u)_I\) satisfies \(r_I=Ad_I\), so that the energy error is

\[
\varepsilon(q)=\frac{d_I^TAd_I}{q^TSq}
=\frac{r_I^TA^{-1}r_I}{q^TSq}.
\tag{7}
\]

The residual can be computed without the exact recovery \(E\). Evaluating its \(A^{-1}\)-norm, however, requires one interior solve per test displacement. A bound on the energy error over all retained displacements follows only from how well the sampled test displacements cover them (Appendices B and G.4). Proposition 1 and the residual form of Eq. (7) are classical results [Fraeijs de Veubeke (1965)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.339), [Toselli & Widlund (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1007/b137868), [Becker & Rannacher (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0962492901000010).

In NICE, \(F=\mathcal W\widehat E\). Section 3.2 constructs the network recovery operator \(\widehat E\), which reproduces the retained displacements, \(J_P\widehat E=I_p\), and the rigid-body motions, \(\widehat ER_P=R\), by construction (Eq. (9)). The correction \(\mathcal W\) is one symmetric two-grid cycle applied with the retained displacements held fixed (Sections 3.3 and 3.4); it is linear and leaves the retained displacements unchanged. Taking \(\mathcal W\) as the identity gives the uncorrected recovery \(F=\widehat E\). For each geometry, the steps of the cycle and their coefficients are fixed independently of the retained displacements. A correction driven by the interior residual, such as \(\mathcal W\), leaves a rigid-body field unchanged (Appendix B.2). The corrected operator \(F\) thus also reproduces the retained displacements and the rigid-body motions, and Proposition 1 applies to it. By Eq. (6), the error of the condensed stiffness is the energy of the interior error of \(F\), which Sections 3.3 and 3.4 reduce. After the assembled system is solved, \(F_mB_m\widehat U\) gives the displacements of cell \(m\) for evaluating the response and the sensitivities.

### 3.2. Network for the interior displacements

The network approximates the interior part of the exact recovery, the map \(q\mapsto E_Iq=-A^{-1}K_{IP}q\); this approximation yields a displacement field and, through its strain energy, the condensed stiffness on all retained DOFs (Section 3.1). Since a cell has 2,679 to 45,900 retained DOFs and this set changes with the cut, two direct approaches are unsuitable: forming the recovery matrix for every geometry requires one interior solve per retained DOF, and a network with one output per retained DOF would need an output dimension that changes with the cut.

The relations of Sections 3.1 and 4 rest on three properties, which the architecture enforces exactly by construction: (i) at fixed geometry the recovery is linear in \(q\), since all nonlinearity is confined to a geometry branch and every operation on the displacement features is linear; (ii) the recovery reproduces the retained displacements and the rigid-body motions, since the rigid-body motion is separated before the network and reconstructed after it, and the retained displacements are overwritten at the output (Eq. (9)); (iii) the transpose of the recovery, which the nodal forces of Eq. (5) require, is obtained by transposing the linear displacement path operation by operation (Appendix G.1). Section 5.2 verifies the three properties numerically.

The remaining design choices determine how well the network approximates the map, and they were not tested individually by ablation. For each active element and each element face on which the ghost penalty acts, the network uses a fixed set of nodes, called a stencil. The weights of the network act on the local node positions of the stencils, on the feature channels and on the grid levels, and the coefficients of each stencil are computed from the geometry. One network therefore serves the sets of retained DOFs of all geometries within the range covered by training. The element moments are the integrals of local monomials over the material part of each background element (Appendix A.1). They enter as geometry input, because the wall surfaces and the cut plane intersect the background elements. The map from the retained displacements to the interior displacements couples distant parts of the cell. A U-shaped grid hierarchy, which passes the features to successively coarser grids and back, carries this long-range coupling. Additional local interactions act on the stencils that contain weakly connected nodes, which are coupled to the rest of the cell by few and small stiffness entries.

The network therefore has two branches (Figure 3): a nonlinear geometry branch computes, once per geometry, the coefficients of a linear operator, and the displacement branch is this linear operator.

The rigid-body motion is separated before the network is applied. The columns of \(R\) are taken as three translations and three rotations about a common centre. The matrix \(C_R=(R_P^TR_P)^{-1}R_P^T\) extracts the rigid-body coefficients, and the projector \(\Pi_P=I_p-R_PC_R\) removes the rigid-body part. Together they split the input into the rigid-body coefficients \(C_Rq\) and the retained deformation \(q_d=\Pi_Pq\). The network acts only on \(q_d\), so the reconstruction of the rigid-body motion does not depend on how well the network is trained.

In the geometry branch, the element moments describe the material distribution within each background element. The node features indicate whether a node carries retained DOFs, whether it belongs to the cut-plane elements and whether it is weakly connected, and also contain the local stiffness magnitude and the node position. A node is weakly connected when the norm of its diagonal stiffness block is below 1% of the median over the active nodes (Appendix G). Encoders map these inputs to element and node feature vectors, which exchange information in two rounds. Coefficient heads, which are small output networks, then compute the geometry-dependent coefficients: the weights of the fixed element–node and ghost-face–node connections, of the transfers between the grids of the hierarchy and of the coarse-grid convolutions. For a given geometry, these coefficients do not depend on the retained displacements.

The displacement branch lifts the three components of \(q_d\) to 32 feature channels on the retained nodes, which carry retained DOFs, and sets the features of the interior nodes to zero. This defines the initial features \(X^0(q)\). Local interactions propagate these values through the neighbourhoods of the elements and of the ghost-penalty faces. Each interaction gathers the features at the 27 local node positions of a stencil with four separate sets of geometry-dependent weights, called heads. It then mixes the channels within each head and scatters the result back to the nodes of the stencil. Let \(\mathsf M_I\) and \(\mathsf M_P\) be the masks of the interior and retained nodes, acting on nodal rows. One interaction updates the features by

\[
X^{\ell+1}=\mathsf M_I\left[X^\ell+
 \sum_h\mathcal S_{\ell h}(\eta)
   \big(\mathcal G_{\ell h}(\eta)X^\ell W_{\ell h}\big)\right]
 +\mathsf M_PX^0(q).
\tag{8}
\]

Here \(\mathcal G_{\ell h}\) and \(\mathcal S_{\ell h}\) are the gather and scatter maps with geometry-dependent weights, \(W_{\ell h}\) mixes the channels, and the last term restores the retained features. The U-shaped grid hierarchy, which provides the long-range coupling, passes through grids with 65, 33, 17 and 9 positions per axis, the finest of which is the \(Q_2\) node grid of the cell. Pairs of local interactions before and after the hierarchy, and further pairs on the stencils that contain weakly connected nodes, complete the branch. A linear map then converts the features to nodal displacements (Figure 3b,c). Because the weights do not act on individual DOFs, the same network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves every set of retained DOFs (Supplementary Table ST15). For a given channel width and stencil size, the work of one network application is proportional to the number of element and ghost-face stencils and to the size of the fixed grids. This work does not depend on how many of the nodes carry retained DOFs. One application processes a batch of retained displacement vectors at once. Appendix G gives the complete order of operations and the definitions of the coefficients.

Every operation on the displacement features is linear, and the nonlinear encoders act only on the geometry. The raw map \(\mathcal N_\theta(\eta)\), with network parameters \(\theta\), therefore obeys superposition at fixed \(\eta\). Adding back the rigid-body field and restoring the prescribed retained displacements gives the network recovery operator

\[
\widehat E q=J_P^Tq+
J_I^TJ_I\left[RC_Rq+\mathcal N_\theta(\eta)\Pi_Pq\right].
\tag{9}
\]

It follows that \(J_P\widehat E=I_p\) and \(\widehat ER_P=R\). The former results from the final overwrite of the retained displacements, and the latter from the separation of the rigid-body motion. Both relations hold by construction. The transpose \(\mathcal N_\theta(\eta)^T\) required by Eq. (5) is the composition of the transposed operations in reverse order (Eq. (E.1)). In exact arithmetic, the returned nodal forces are therefore the derivative of the strain energy \(\widehat{\mathcal U}\).

The data flow of the displacement branch resembles that of an iterative solver for the interior problem, in which the retained displacements are prescribed (Dirichlet) boundary values. Each local interaction of Eq. (8) updates the interior features from their stencil neighbours with geometry-dependent coefficients and, at the same time, resets the retained features to their lifted prescribed values. The grid hierarchy carries information between distant parts of the cell, as the coarse levels do in multigrid. The resemblance is limited to the data flow. The branch never applies the stiffness matrix and does not compute residuals, nor is the equilibrium solution of the interior problem a fixed point of its layers. The branch is a fixed sequence of learned linear maps. The two-grid correction of Sections 3.3 and 3.4, in contrast, acts on the residual of the same interior problem with prescribed coefficients. Because the network is trained with this correction applied (Section 3.5), it provides the part of the interior displacement field that a fixed number of correction steps does not recover.

### 3.3. Chebyshev smoothing

Equation (7) expresses the energy error of a cell through its interior residual, and Eq. (14) expresses the compliance error as the energy of the error of the recovered field. Both suggest reducing the interior force imbalance of the network prediction while the retained displacements are held fixed. Polynomial smoothing [Adams et al. (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0021-9991(03)00194-3) and energy minimisation on a coarse grid reduce different components of this imbalance.

Let \(D=\operatorname{diag}(A)\) be the diagonal of the interior stiffness matrix \(A\). The generalised eigenvectors \(v_j\) satisfy \(Av_j=\lambda_jDv_j\) and \(v_j^TDv_k=\delta_{jk}\), and they are ordered by increasing eigenvalue. With the coefficients \(c_j=v_j^TDd_I\) of the interior error \(d_I\) of Section 3.1, the error energy and its fraction in the first \(\ell\) modes are

\[
d_I^TAd_I=\sum_j\lambda_jc_j^2,\qquad
\chi_\ell(q)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{\ell}\lambda_jc_j^2}{d_I^TAd_I}.
\tag{10}
\]

The fraction \(\chi_\ell\) gives the part of the error energy that lies in the first \(\ell\) Jacobi-scaled interior modes. The corresponding fraction for the exact interior field \(u_I\) is defined in the same way, with \(u_I^TAu_I\) as its total energy.

The network prediction is an initial approximation to the solution of \(Au_I=-K_{IP}q\). Chebyshev semi-iteration with Jacobi preconditioning [Golub & Varga (1961)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01386013) improves this approximation with \(q\) held fixed. The number of iterations is prescribed, and the coefficients depend on the geometry but not on \(q\), so the corrected recovery operator remains linear. Smoothing a learned initial approximation in this way resembles the smoothing of a tentative prolongation in smoothed aggregation [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511). It also resembles hybrid solvers in which relaxation removes the high-frequency error that a learned prediction leaves [Zhang, E. et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s42256-024-00910-x).

After \(k\) smoothing steps, the interior error \(d_I\) becomes \(\Phi_kd_I\), where \(\Phi_k=p_k(D^{-1}A)\) and \(p_k\) is the error polynomial of degree \(k\). Smoothing therefore multiplies each coefficient \(c_j\) in Eq. (10) by \(p_k(\lambda_j)\). The Chebyshev polynomial is constructed for a target interval \([a,b]\) with \(0<a<b\). It does not increase the error in the \(A\)-energy norm if the actual positive spectrum of \(D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2}\) lies in \((0,b]\). Modes with eigenvalues below \(a\) can decay slowly, which motivates the complementary coarse-grid correction of Section 3.4. The correction sequences in this paper use \(a=b/30\), where \(b\) is a power-iteration estimate of the largest eigenvalue of \(D^{-1}A\), increased by 5%. Section 5.2 verifies that \(b\) bounds the spectrum. Appendix D gives the polynomial, its recurrence, the estimate of the interval and the contraction bound.

### 3.4. Coarse-grid correction and the two-grid cycle

Let \(\widehat u_I\) be the current approximation of the interior displacements, either the network prediction or a smoothed iterate, and let \(r_I=(K\widehat u)_I\) be its interior residual. The coarse basis \(V\in\mathbb R^{i\times n_c}\) is assumed to have full column rank; Appendix F.1 treats a basis without it. With the coarse-grid matrix \(A_c=V^TAV\) and \(b_r=V^Tr_I\), minimising the energy over \(\widehat u_I+\operatorname{range}V\) gives

\[
\widehat u_I^{\rm c}=\widehat u_I-VA_c^{-1}b_r,\qquad
d_I^{\rm c}=(I_i-VA_c^{-1}V^TA)d_I,\qquad
\|d_I\|_A^2-\|d_I^{\rm c}\|_A^2=b_r^TA_c^{-1}b_r.
\tag{11}
\]

The coarse-grid correction enforces interior equilibrium for every virtual displacement in the coarse space. The last relation in Eq. (11) gives the energy removed by this projection [Xu (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1137/1034116). The principal coarse basis, denoted \(Q_1(17)\), consists of trilinear vector functions on a grid of \(17^3\) vertices, restricted to the interior DOFs (Appendix F.1). All coarse spaces act on the interior DOFs only, so the retained displacements stay fixed.

The two-grid cycle places the coarse-grid correction between two smoothing stages of \(k\) steps each. This cycle is the correction \(\mathcal W\) of Section 3.1. Let \(C_V=I_i-VA_c^{-1}V^TA\) be the error operator of the coarse-grid correction, and let \(H_{\rm net}=J_I(\widehat E-E)\) be the interior error of the network recovery operator. The two-grid error operator \(\Phi_kC_V\Phi_k\) [Hackbusch (1985)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-02427-0), [Trottenberg et al. (2001)](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/multigrid/trottenberg/978-0-12-701070-0), [Xu & Zikatanov (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0894-0347-02-00398-3), [Falgout et al. (2005)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nla.437) maps \(H_{\rm net}\) to the interior error of \(F\). By Eq. (6), the error of the condensed stiffness is then

\[
H=\Phi_kC_V\Phi_kH_{\rm net},\qquad
\widehat S-S=H^TAH.
\tag{12}
\]

**Proposition 2 (correction ordering).** Let the network recovery operator \(\widehat E\) satisfy (A2), let the coarse solve be exact or use the coarse inverse with the nonnegative shift of Appendix F.1 (Eq. (D.6)), and let \(\|\Phi_k\|_A\le1\). Then, under (A1), \(S\preceq\widehat S\preceq\widehat S_{\rm net}\), where \(\widehat S_{\rm net}=\widehat E^TK\widehat E\) is the condensed stiffness without correction (Appendix D.1). The same ordering holds for any linear correction that is fixed for a given geometry and whose interior error map \(\Theta\) satisfies \(\Theta^TA\Theta\preceq A\).

The correction therefore cannot increase the error in the \(A\)-energy norm. The assembled stiffness matrices inherit the ordering of Proposition 2, and their inverses are ordered in reverse (Appendix C), so the correction moves the assembled compliance towards the reference value (Appendix D.1). Proposition 2 guarantees the ordering but not the size of the reduction (Appendix D). Section 5.5 measures this reduction.

**Remark 1.** The guarantee of Proposition 2 rests on the two conditions on the smoothing and on the coarse solve, and it covers the energy only. If the spectrum of \(D^{-1}A\) extends above \(a+b\), the Chebyshev polynomial amplifies these modes, and the cycle can increase the energy error (Appendix D). Changing the number \(k\) of smoothing steps changes the polynomial. The ordering therefore holds for each \(k\) separately, and the reduction need not be monotone in \(k\). For an approximate coarse inverse \(G\), the correction does not increase the energy error if \(2G-GA_cG\succeq0\) (Eq. (D.6)). The nonnegative shift satisfies this condition, whereas an arbitrary approximation need not. The ordering does not apply to the sensitivity. In the example of Appendix D.1, a projection halves the energy of the interior error. The same projection changes the sensitivity difference \(\widetilde s_c-s_c\) of Eq. (17) from zero to \(2t-t^2\), whereas the reference sensitivity is \(-1\).

### 3.5. Training

The network is trained on sets of test displacements. Prescribed polynomial and multiscale displacements cover a broad range of spatial variation. Responses to equilibrated nodal point loads, traction loads and spring supports, together with displacements imposed by a neighbouring cell, provide test displacements that arise from mechanical loading. The rigid-body components are removed, and each test displacement is normalised with the exact condensed stiffness to \(q_j^TSq_j=1\). For a batch of \(B\) test displacements, the loss function is

\[
\mathcal L(\theta)=\frac1B\sum_{j=1}^{B}\log(q_j^T\widehat S q_j)
 +w_s\frac1{|\mathcal J_s|}\sum_{j\in\mathcal J_s}
 \frac{\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}_j-\boldsymbol s_j\|_2^2}{\|\boldsymbol s_j\|_2^2}.
\tag{13}
\]

Because \(q_j^TSq_j=1\), the first term is the mean logarithm of the energy ratio \(q_j^T\widehat Sq_j/(q_j^TSq_j)\). By Proposition 1, for a recovery operator that reproduces the retained displacements, this term is smallest when the recovered field equals the exact field for every sampled test displacement. The second term compares the thickness sensitivities \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}_j\) computed from the recovered field (Section 4.2) with the reference sensitivities \(\boldsymbol s_j\). Both are eight-component vectors, and the term is evaluated on the subset \(\mathcal J_s\) of test displacements that have reference sensitivities. All trained networks reported in this paper use \(w_s=1\). The gradients of the loss with respect to the network parameters \(\theta\) pass through the recovered field to the geometry encoders, the coefficient heads and the linear displacement maps. Test displacements with large energy ratios are added during training. They are found by a block search on the complement of the rigid-body modes, which iterates eight candidate test displacements at once (Appendix G.3). The geometries are augmented with the 48 symmetries of the cube (Appendix G). Table 2 lists the variants, and Supplementary Table ST01 gives the settings for training and model selection.

NICE is trained with the operator that is used at deployment. The loss of Eq. (13) is evaluated with the corrected recovery operator \(F=\mathcal W\widehat E\), where \(\mathcal W\) is the two-grid cycle of Table 1. This cycle is written 8 / \(Q_1(17)\) / 8, which denotes 8 pre-smoothing steps, the coarse-grid correction with \(Q_1(17)\) and 8 post-smoothing steps. Because \(\mathcal W\) is linear and fixed for a given geometry, the gradient passes through the smoothing recurrence and the coarse-grid correction to the network parameters, as in solver-in-the-loop training [Um et al. (2020)](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2020/hash/43e4e6a6f341e00671e123714de019a8-Abstract.html). The smoothing interval and the factorisation of the coarse-grid matrix depend only on \(K\) and are recomputed for each geometry, so the correction adds no trainable parameters.

### 3.6. Applying the condensed stiffness

The quantities that depend on the geometry are computed once for each geometry and reused for all retained displacements. They are the element moments, the geometry encoding of the network with its coefficients and maps, the smoothing interval and the factorisation of the coarse-grid matrix. Supplementary Table ST13 gives the cost of this preparation. Each product of \(\widehat S\) with a vector of retained displacements then requires one forward pass of the network, one two-grid cycle and one product with \(K\). It also requires the transposes of the cycle and of the network (Appendices E–F). Neither \(\widehat E\) nor \(\widehat S\) is formed as a matrix. Each cell stores its geometry-dependent coefficients and the factorisation of its coarse-grid matrix. By comparison, a dense \(\widehat S\) for a cell with the median number of retained DOFs, 23,604, would take 4.2 GiB in double precision.

**Algorithm 1. Action of the corrected condensed stiffness.**

1. Compute \(\widehat Eq\) with the network, and apply the two-grid cycle \(\mathcal W\) with the retained displacements held fixed to obtain \(\widehat u=Fq\).
2. Compute \(y=K\widehat u\) and return \(F^Ty\) by applying \(\mathcal W^T\) and then the transpose of the network recovery operator.
3. Assemble these cell actions through Eq. (3). After the global solve, recover the cell displacements \(F_mB_m\widehat U\) to evaluate the displacement field and the sensitivities.

## 4. Effect of interior displacement errors on compliance and sensitivities

Because all retained DOFs are kept, a cell is approximated only through its interior displacements. This section traces the error of a recovery operator through the condensed stiffness to the two quantities used in design, the compliance and the sensitivities. The recovery operator may be any operator that satisfies assumption (A2) of Section 2.4. Proposition 1 in Section 3.1 shows that the condensed stiffness exceeds the exact condensed stiffness by the energy of the interior error. The condensed stiffness is therefore never softer than the exact one, and its error is quadratic in the interior displacement error and can be computed from the interior residual (Eq. (7)). Section 4.1 shows that the error reaches the assembled compliance weighted by the fraction of the strain energy that each cell carries. Section 4.2 shows that it reaches the thickness sensitivity through the derivative of the stiffness matrix, with a term linear in the interior displacement error. An accurate compliance therefore does not imply an accurate local sensitivity.

The relations are stated for a single substructure and apply to every cell of an assembly. Proposition 1 and the residual form of Eq. (7) are classical (Section 3.1), and so is the self-adjoint compliance sensitivity [Haftka & Gürdal (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-011-2550-5), [Bendsøe & Sigmund (2004)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-662-05086-6). The bound weighted by the energy fractions and the sensitivity relations are derived in this paper for approximate recovery operators. Propositions 3 and 4 use the assumptions of Section 2.4.

### 4.1. Compliance

Consider the exact solutions of the reference and the approximate assembled systems of Eq. (3). Both systems use the same cell stiffness matrices, assembly maps and homogeneous supports, and the same nonzero load on the retained DOFs. Let \(u_m=E_mB_mU\) and \(\widehat u_m=F_mB_m\widehat U\) be the displacements of cell \(m\) recovered from the two solutions, and let \(a(v,v)=\sum_m v_m^TK_mv_m\) be the energy form of the assembly. Global equilibrium and the energy orthogonality within each cell then give (Appendix C)

\[
C-\widehat C
=a(\widehat u-u,\widehat u-u)
=\|\widehat U-U\|_{\mathbb K}^{2}
+\sum_m\|H_mB_m\widehat U\|_{A_m}^{2}.
\tag{14}
\]

The compliance is therefore underestimated by the total energy of the error of the recovered displacement field. This energy is the sum of two orthogonal parts. The first comes from the change of the assembled retained solution, and the second from the departure of the interior displacements from equilibrium at that solution. The part of a cell's error that reaches the compliance depends on the strain energy that the cell carries. At the exact assembled retained displacements \(q_m=B_mU\), the energy fraction of cell \(m\) is \(w_m=q_m^TS_mq_m/C\), and these fractions sum to one. The weighted energy error is \(\beta=\sum_mw_m\varepsilon_m(q_m)\), where \(\varepsilon_m\) is the energy error of cell \(m\) as defined in Eq. (7). A cell whose response is a rigid-body motion has zero energy, and its contribution to \(\beta\) is taken as zero.

**Proposition 3 (energy-weighted compliance error).** Under (A1)–(A3), the relative compliance error is bounded by the weighted energy error \(\beta\) (Appendix C.1):

\[
\boxed{0\le\frac{C-\widehat C}{C}\le\frac{\beta}{1+\beta}\le\beta.}
\tag{15}
\]

A cell with a small energy fraction can therefore leave the compliance accurate even when its own interior displacements are inaccurate.

Eq. (14) also separates the error of the condensed stiffness from the error of an incomplete assembled solve, and the numerical checks of Section 5.8 use this separation. Let \(\bar U\) be an approximate solution of the assembled system, with the recomputed residual \(\rho=f_g-\widehat{\mathbb K}\bar U\) and the recovered cell displacements \(\bar u_m=F_mB_m\bar U\). Then

\[
C-f_g^T\bar U=a(\bar u-u,\bar u-u)+\bar U^T\rho,
\tag{16}
\]

and the signed residual work \(\bar U^T\rho\) measures the effect of stopping the solve early (Appendix C.2).

### 4.2. Sensitivities

Consider a differentiable design interval on which the active and retained DOFs, the assembly maps and the homogeneous supports are fixed, and on which the load does not depend on the design. On such an interval, the exact compliance sensitivity is \(s_c=-u^TK_{,c}u\), where \(K_{,c}=\partial K/\partial\tau_c\). For a parameter that affects several substructures, their contributions are summed. The design derivative of the exact recovery \(E\) does not appear in \(s_c\), because the exact field is in interior equilibrium [Giles & Pierce (2000)](https://doi.org/10.1023/a:1011430410075), [Haftka & Gürdal (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-94-011-2550-5). The sensitivity computed from the recovered field is \(\widetilde s_c=-\widehat u^TK_{,c}\widehat u\), with the same stiffness derivative. It ignores the dependence of the recovery operator \(F\) on the design, and hence the design dependence of the network. Over the eight corners, \(s_c\) and \(\widetilde s_c\) form the vectors \(\boldsymbol s\) and \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}\).

**Proposition 4 (sensitivity error).** Under (A1), (A2) and (D), with both sensitivities evaluated at the same retained displacements:

(a) the sensitivity \(\widetilde s_c\) differs from the exact sensitivity by a term linear and a term quadratic in the interior displacement error,

\[
\boxed{\widetilde s_c-s_c=-2d^TK_{,c}u-d^TK_{,c}d,
\qquad u=Eq,\quad d=Fq-Eq;}
\tag{17}
\]

(b) with \(\mathcal E=d^TKd\) the energy of the interior error, \(\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}-\boldsymbol s\|_2\le2L\sqrt{\mathcal E}+Q\mathcal E\), where the constants \(L\) and \(Q\) of Eq. (H.4) depend on the cell, the test displacement and the stiffness derivatives. The energy error therefore controls the relative sensitivity error only at the order \(\sqrt\varepsilon\) (Appendix H.1).

Interior equilibrium gives \((Ku)_I=0\), but in general \((K_{,c}u)_I\ne0\). The linear cross term therefore remains, and a decrease of the energy error need not decrease the sensitivity error monotonically. Thickening a corner enlarges the material domain, so that \(K_{,c}\succeq0\) under exact integration (Eq. (H.7)). The quadratic term of Eq. (17) is then nonpositive, whereas the linear term can have either sign (Appendix H.2).

The sensitivity \(\widetilde s_c\) is not the derivative of the approximate compliance \(\widehat C\), because \(\widehat C\) also depends on the design through \(F\). For a parameter that affects one substructure, differentiation with the retained DOFs fixed gives

\[
\widehat C_{,c}
=-\widehat u^TK_{,c}\widehat u
 -2\widehat q^TF_{,c}^TK\widehat u
=\widetilde s_c-2(F_{I,c}\widehat q)^Tr_I,
\qquad\widehat u=F\widehat q,
\tag{18}
\]

where \(\widehat q\) is the assembled retained solution obtained with \(\widehat S\), \(r_I=(KF\widehat q)_I\) and \(F_{I,c}=J_I\partial F/\partial\tau_c\). The second term vanishes when the recovered field is in interior equilibrium, but energy accuracy alone does not control it. Whether Eq. (18) or \(\widetilde s_c\) gives the more accurate gradient depends on whether \(\|H_{,c}q\|_A\) is small compared with \(\|E_{I,c}q\|_A\) (Eq. (H.6)). The quantities \(H_{,c}\) and \(E_{I,c}=J_IE_{,c}\) are the design derivatives of the interior error of \(F\) and of the exact interior recovery (Appendix H.2). All sensitivities reported in this paper are \(\widetilde s_c=-\widehat u^TK_{,c}\widehat u\), which estimate the exact sensitivity and ignore the design dependence of the network. The derivatives in this section hold on intervals on which the discrete choices of Appendix B.1 are fixed. Across a switch of the discrete model, the reference compliance itself can jump. Any jump that the approximate compliance adds is bounded by its own error level, because \(0\le C-\widehat C\le\beta C\) holds at every design by Eq. (15) (Supplementary Note S4.3). Appendix H specifies the numerical stiffness derivatives.

These relations imply that a condensed stiffness based on network-predicted interior displacements must be checked separately for the compliance and for the sensitivities. By Proposition 3, the relative compliance error is bounded by the energy errors of the cells at the exact assembled retained displacements, weighted by their energy fractions. By Proposition 4, the energy error of a cell controls its relative sensitivity error only at the order \(\sqrt\varepsilon\). Section 5.6 checks both quantities in two-cell assemblies, and Section 5.10 checks them in thickness designs.

## 5. Numerical examples

The numerical examples cover single cells, assemblies of cells and thickness design. On single cells, the mean energy error of NICE over the 80 validation geometries is 0.074%. Applying the correction to the base network without retraining lowers the mean energy error under traction loads from 6.89% to 0.0965%. Under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field without trainable parameters leaves 5.4 to 265 times the error of NICE (Sections 5.3–5.5). After assembly, the compliance error follows the relation of Proposition 3, in which the energy error of each cell is weighted by its energy fraction; the local sensitivity, however, has to be checked separately (Sections 5.6–5.8). In design, an analysis of an eight-cell lattice with sensitivities is about ten times faster than a direct solution of the whole lattice (Section 5.9). For the optimised designs, the compliance and the gradient computed with NICE agree with those of exact static condensation (Section 5.10). Table 3 summarises the accuracy of NICE against exact static condensation in all assembled examples. Sections 5.1 and 5.2 give the settings of the examples and verify the reference model.

### 5.2. Verification of the reference model and of the deployed operator

The reference model with \(n=32\) was verified by mesh refinement on seven cells. U1 was refined to \(n=40\), and M1 and M2 to \(n=48\) (Figure S01). H1, H2 and two further validation cells were refined to \(n=64\) (Supplementary Table ST10). Except in the heavily cut cell H2, refinement changes the compliance by at most 0.28% and the thickness sensitivities by at most 0.44%; in H2, the changes reach about 1% in the compliance and 3.6% in the sensitivity. On uncut cells and assemblies of uncut cells, the compliance of the discrete model agrees with that of an independent body-fitted discretisation with quadratic tetrahedra to 0.05–0.25% (Supplementary Note S1). Varying the ghost-penalty coefficient between \(10^{-5}\) and \(10^{-3}\) and refining the volume integration each change the compliance and the sensitivities by at most 0.12%. Supplementary Note S1 also verifies the finite-difference stiffness derivative and its step size (Figure S01(c)). All subsequent comparisons are made against this discrete reference model (Section 2.1).

On five cells, U2, M1, M2, H1 and H2, the deployed operator is symmetric and satisfies the work–energy identity, both to relative deviations of at most \(3\times10^{-8}\). This identity states that the work of the returned nodal forces on the retained displacements equals the energy of the recovered field. The deployed operator gives the rigid-body modes zero energy up to round-off. Its recovered field agrees with the field computed by the training implementation to within \(1.1\times10^{-7}\) (Supplementary Table ST04).

The Chebyshev smoothing of Section 3.3 is guaranteed not to increase the energy error when \(b\ge\lambda_{\max}(D^{-1}A)\). On all 80 validation geometries, the endpoint \(b\) obtained from the power iteration exceeds the converged largest eigenvalue by 2.1–5.0% (Appendix D). The Gershgorin bound is a guaranteed upper bound, but it is 4.7 to 19.4 times larger than \(b\), with a median of 18.4. Used as \(b\), it would place the smoothing interval far above the actual spectrum. On these geometries, the comparison with the converged largest eigenvalue supports the spectral condition numerically. It was not checked for the cells of the lattices and optimisations of Sections 5.8–5.10, which use the same power-iteration estimate. The symmetry and positive semidefiniteness of \(\widehat S\) and the ordering \(\widehat S\succeq S\) do not depend on this condition.

### 5.3. Accuracy on single cells

In every cut-severity group, the mean energy error of NICE is about 0.1% or less (Figure 5), and under traction loads its group means are lower than those of the base network by factors of 71 to 117. The error of NICE still grows about sevenfold from uncut to heavily cut cells: its group means rise from 0.016% to 0.109%, whereas those of the base network rise from 1.14% to 12.7% (Supplementary Table ST03b). Over all 80 geometries, the mean energy errors of NICE and the base network are 0.074% and 6.89%, and their largest single-geometry means are 0.65% and 48.6%, respectively. Over the 5,120 sampled test displacements under traction loads, 99% of the energy errors of NICE lie below 0.69%, and the largest is 1.24% (Supplementary Table ST03).

Under nodal point loads, the mean energy error of the base network rises from 0.908% for uncut to 11.3% for heavily cut cells, and its largest single-geometry mean is 70.1%. The group means of NICE under these loads stay between 0.013% and 0.090%. Under the other load classes, the means of NICE are of the same order (Supplementary Table ST03). Figure S02 shows the distributions over the geometries. On the 60 geometries outside checkpoint selection, the class means of NICE differ from those over all 80 geometries by at most 0.005 percentage points. Under traction loads, the mean of NICE for the heavily cut group is higher on these 60 geometries, 0.126% against 0.109%. The five largest single-geometry means of NICE under traction loads all belong to these 60 geometries (Supplementary Table ST03).

Most of the improvement comes from the correction itself. Applying the correction to the base network at deployment, without retraining, lowers the mean under traction loads from 6.89% to 0.0965%, with a largest single-geometry mean of 0.91%. Continuing the training of the base network without the correction leaves its mean nearly unchanged (Supplementary Note S8). NICE also continues the training, with the correction inside the training loop, and draws its training geometries from a larger set. This continuation lowers the mean by a further factor of 1.31, with a 95% bootstrap interval over the geometries of 1.20–1.40 (Supplementary Table ST03). Under the same correction, a deterministic graph-harmonic initial field leaves 5.4 to 265 times the error of NICE (Section 5.5).

### 5.4. Where the network error lies

Part of the error left by the network lies in modes that smoothing damps slowly, and part lies next to the cut. Relative to its amplitude, the error also carries far more strain energy than the exact solution; in this sense it is stiffer. This section examines the error before the correction: its distribution over the interior spectrum and in space, the reason why an interior displacement error of about 1% produces an energy error of several percent, and the split of its effect on the sensitivity between the linear and the quadratic term of Eq. (17).

The error of the base network occupies different parts of the Jacobi-scaled interior spectrum in different cells (Figure 6). In M1 under traction loads, the lowest 200 modes contain 24.5% of the error energy but only 4.87% of the energy of the exact field (Supplementary Table ST05b). All 200 modes lie below the lower endpoint \(a=0.173\) of the smoothing interval, with \(a=b/30\) (Table 1). In H2, only 66 of the 200 modes lie below \(a=0.138\). Smoothing damps these modes slowly and leaves them to the coarse-grid correction (Section 5.5).

Part of the error of the base network lies next to the cut plane (Figure 7). In M1, the elements within two element widths of the cut plane make up 13% of the elements. For the traction-load test displacement plotted, the exact field places 8% of its energy in these elements. Over the four test displacements evaluated, 39–43% of the error energy of the base network lies in this layer. The layer adjoins the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements, whose values the network must propagate into the interior. Relative to the base network, NICE reduces the total error energy by about two orders of magnitude, by factors of 113 to 138, and the element error energies by factors of roughly 25 to 300 (10th to 90th percentiles). It also halves the fraction of the error energy in this layer, to 19–22%.

A small displacement error produces a large energy error when the error is stiff. To measure this amplification, let \(d_I\) be the interior displacement error and \(u_I\) the exact interior displacements. Let \(\delta^2=d_I^TDd_I/u_I^TDu_I\) be the Jacobi-weighted relative displacement error. Let \(\kappa=(d_I^TAd_I/d_I^TDd_I)/(u^TKu/u_I^TDu_I)\) be the ratio of the Rayleigh quotients of the interior error and of the exact field. Then \(\varepsilon=\delta^2\kappa\) holds identically (Appendix B.3). Under traction loads, the base network has mean \(\delta\) of 0.7–1.3% in M1, M2, H1 and H2, but mean \(\kappa\) of 124 to 7089 (Supplementary Table ST04). The displacement error is therefore small, but relative to its amplitude it is far stiffer than the exact field, and the resulting energy errors are 1.8–35%. In the uncut cell U2, a similar \(\delta\) of 0.74% combines with \(\kappa=85\) and gives an energy error of 0.40%. For NICE, \(\delta\) is 0.05–0.19% and \(\kappa\) is 33 to 582 in the cut cells, and the two values are 0.11% and 47 in U2.

Where the errors of the base network are large, the quadratic term of Eq. (17) dominates the sensitivity error. In M1 and H2, the sensitivity errors at fixed retained displacements are 14.1% and 75.1% under traction loads, and the linear term contributes only 7.2% and 0.79%, respectively, of the sum of the norms of the linear and quadratic terms. Under traction loads, the linear term contributes 28–43% in the three cells whose energy errors are below 2%, namely U1, U2 and H1. Under nodal point loads, it contributes 24–72% in all six cells. Because the linear term is first order in the displacement error, it dominates as this error tends to zero (Supplementary Table ST05 and Figure S03).

### 5.5. Correction of a fixed network

With the base network held fixed, the effect of the correction can be examined in isolation (Figure 8). Eight Chebyshev smoothing steps at fixed retained displacements reduce the mean energy error in every cell, but unevenly (Supplementary Table ST05a). Under traction loads, the error of H2 falls from 35.0% to 0.205%. The error of M1 falls only from 13.5% to 4.69%, and to 2.95% after 32 steps, because much of it lies in modes below the smoothing interval (Section 5.4). The sensitivity error of M1 falls from 14.1% to 2.76%. In U1, the energy error decreases at every step, whereas the sensitivity error varies non-monotonically with the number of steps (Figure 8a,b).

A trilinear coarse-grid correction applied before the same eight steps reduces the error of M1 to 0.230%, and eight additional pre-smoothing steps reduce it further to 0.186%. The coarse space of M1 has 5,601 DOFs for 165,927 interior DOFs. With two, four and eight steps per smoothing stage, the two-grid cycle gives 1.02%, 0.422% and 0.186% on M1. The eight-step cycle gives about 0.027% on M2 and U1 (Supplementary Table ST07). Supplementary Table ST06 compares other coarse spaces.

Table 4 applies the same correction, at the same retained displacements, to four initial fields: zero interior displacements, a graph-harmonic initial field, the field of the base network and the field of NICE. The graph-harmonic field is computed with a volume-weighted graph Laplacian on the element connectivity, after the same exact separation of the rigid-body part as for the network. ### 5.6. Two-cell assemblies: compliance and sensitivity

This section checks whether the small single-cell energy errors of Sections 5.3–5.5 give an accurate compliance and accurate local sensitivities after assembly. Each two-cell assembly joins a learned test cell to an exact, uncut neighbouring cell, and the wall thickness is continuous across their interface (Figure 9). The seven test cells, U1, U2, L1, M1, M2, H1 and H3, belong to the geometries used for checkpoint selection (Section 5.1). Each test cell is assembled in two configurations, x and y, and 'M1/x' denotes test cell M1 in configuration x. Each configuration has six face loads: traction loads in the three Cartesian directions, applied separately on the loaded face of the test cell and on that of the neighbouring cell. For each load, the compliance and the eight-component thickness-sensitivity vector of each cell are compared with their exact values. The nodal load is held at its value for the base design, and Supplementary Note S4.2 gives the load normalisation and the solver used for the pairs. A common reference tolerance of 3% is used for the compliance and for the sensitivity vector of each cell, and it is drawn as the 3% line in the figures. The reported errors are maxima over the six loads and, for the sensitivity, also over both cells. Three traction loads on the cut surface of a test cell are considered separately.

Under the z traction load on the neighbour face of U1/x, where the test cell carries a small energy fraction, the two errors differ by four orders of magnitude. ### 5.7. Ablation: restricting the cell-face displacements

Keeping all retained DOFs matters more for the local sensitivity than for the compliance. To isolate this effect, pairs in configuration x are solved with the exact condensed stiffness of each cell, while the cell-face displacements of each cell are restricted to tensor-product Bernstein polynomials of degree \(r\). The DOFs of the cut-plane elements that do not lie on the cell faces remain unrestricted. The ablation restricts only the representation of the retained displacements of the present cells, at a fixed cell size. Learned substructures with a reduced boundary control the resulting error by refining the partition, enriching the boundary interpolation or oversampling [Huang et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmps.2024.105893), [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955), [Guo et al. (2026b)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22019v1). These remedies are not reproduced here (Supplementary Note S2). With every cell face restricted, degree one gives compliance errors of 78–85% on U1, M1, M2 and H1, which decrease to about 4% at \(r=5\) and to 0.49–0.74% at \(r=8\). At \(r=8\), the H1 pair has 14,001 retained DOFs instead of 32,991 (Figure 12).

The local sensitivity converges more slowly. At \(r=8\), the test-cell sensitivity error is 1.3–4.5% under the loads on the test-cell face and 24–64% when the loads on the neighbour face are included. Restricting only the shared interface removes most of the compliance error; at \(r=3\), the compliance error on U1, M1 and M2 is then 0.17–0.44%. Under the neighbour-face loads, however, the sensitivity error remains 8–21% at \(r=3\) and 2.2–3.4% at \(r=5\). A restricted interface is therefore adequate for the compliance at low degree, but not for the local sensitivity under neighbour-face loads. Keeping all retained DOFs removes this part of the error, at the cost of a larger assembled model (Supplementary Table ST11).

### 5.8. Lattices of learned cells

In a design, every cell is a learned cell represented by NICE, so the errors of neighbouring cells enter the same assembled solution. Two lattices with a continuously graded thickness field and a planar boundary cut test whether these errors accumulate. The first is a \(2\times2\times2\) block of eight distinct cells, four of which are cut, with remaining volume fractions of 62% and 25%. The second is a \(3\times3\times1\) layer of eight cells, three of which are cut, with one corner position left empty. Neighbouring cells share the corner parameters of their common faces. The corner parameters of the block range from 0.25 to 0.54. The sixteen cells were generated for these examples and were used neither in training nor in checkpoint selection. Each lattice is clamped on one face. It is loaded by unit traction loads on the opposite face in the three Cartesian directions, called the face loads, and by three random load vectors. For the sensitivities, the nodal load is held at its value for the evaluated design (Appendix H). The reference solution assembles the dense exact condensed stiffness matrices and is solved to a recomputed relative residual of at most \(1.3\times10^{-10}\). The lattices of learned cells are solved by preconditioned conjugate gradients on the free retained DOFs, with a balanced two-level preconditioner (Supplementary Note S4.1). Its fine-level action is the inverse of the assembled stiffness block of the retained DOFs, \(\mathbb K_{PP}=\sum_mB_m^TK_{PP,m}B_m\), which is factorised once per design iteration. Its coarse space consists of the trilinear lattice-vertex functions multiplied by the six rigid-body modes.

The errors of the cells do not accumulate in these lattices. Under the face loads, the compliance error stays below 0.015% in both lattices, and the sensitivity error of every cell is at most 0.144% (Table 3). The sensitivities of the cells, summed at the shared corner parameters, give the lattice gradient. Under the face loads, this gradient agrees with the exact one to within 0.09%, with cosines above 0.999999. Over all six loads, the assembled retained solutions differ from the exact ones by 0.13% and 0.12% in the relative Euclidean norm. These lattice errors are smaller than the largest errors of the two-cell assemblies of Section 5.6 because Eq. (15) bounds the compliance error of the lattice by the weighted energy error \(\beta\). In \(\beta\), the energy error of each cell at the exact retained displacements is weighted by the energy fraction of the cell. Under the face loads, these cell energy errors range from 0.006% to 0.026% in the block and from 0.005% to 0.052% in the layer; under the random loads, they range from 0.02% to 0.12%. They are largest in the cut cells. Under the face loads, \(\beta\) exceeds the compliance error of the lattice by less than 0.4% of \(\beta\); under the random loads, the excess is about 4–5%. The excess arises because the assembled solution adapts to the stiffer learned cells, whereas \(\beta\) evaluates the cell errors at the exact retained displacements.

The recomputed residual of these solves stagnates, but the errors above are errors of the condensed stiffness and not of the incomplete solve. The solves reach the prescribed tolerance on the recursively updated residual. The recomputed residual \(\|f_g-\widehat{\mathbb K}\bar U\|/\|f_g\|\), however, stagnates at \(3.6\times10^{-4}\) in the block and at \(3.2\times10^{-3}\) in the layer. This level is consistent with rounding in the single-precision network and does not come from the correction: running the correction in single instead of double precision changes this level by less than 1% in the block. At the final iterate, the residual work \(\bar U^T\rho\) of Eq. (16) is at most \(2.5\times10^{-8}\) of the compliance. Supplementary Note S4.3 also records a dual-norm bound on the residual work and the action–energy inconsistency (Supplementary Table ST14). The latter is the difference between the energy computed with the applied condensed stiffness and the energy of the recovered displacements. Together with the residual work, these records show that the errors above do not come from the incomplete solve.

### 5.9. Computational cost

A lattice analysis with sensitivities by NICE needs less time and memory than the direct solution of the whole lattice and than conventional exact static condensation. It also needs less time than conjugate gradients on the whole lattice preconditioned by algebraic multigrid. The reason is that NICE solves the assembled problem on the retained DOFs only. For the two eight-cell lattices, these number 139,002 and 143,685, whereas the whole-lattice model has 1.83 and 2.11 million DOFs. NICE also prepares every cell by a network evaluation and a fixed correction instead of a factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix. At the scale of Section 5.10, the cut finite element model of a 110-cell plate has 32.7 million DOFs, and its assembled retained system has 1.6 million. A design iteration of this plate takes 19 min, about 11 s per cell. The cost comparison below concerns one lattice analysis with sensitivities, from cell setup to the solution. It uses the two eight-cell lattices of Section 5.8 and two four-cell lattices, which are the \(2\times2\times1\) layers of the block. Each layer has two uncut cells and two cut cells with remaining volume fractions of 62% and 25%. Each layer keeps the retained DOFs, the clamp and the loads of the block. Table 5 compares three routes.

Route (a) is the direct solution of the whole lattice. It assembles the full cut finite element model of the lattice, which contains the retained and interior DOFs of every cell. This model is factorised once by the sparse Cholesky factorisation of MKL PARDISO, with 16 threads on one AMD EPYC 9654 CPU. Its recomputed relative residuals are below \(3\times10^{-9}\), so route (a) also serves as the exact reference for the four-cell lattices.

Route (b) is conventional exact static condensation on the same CPU with 16 threads. Each cell is condensed by a Cholesky factorisation of its interior stiffness matrix with the Schur-complement option of PARDISO. The assembled condensed system is then solved by a block Cholesky factorisation. This factorisation eliminates the retained DOFs that belong to a single cell before the DOFs of the shared interface. The compliance of route (b) agrees with that of route (a) to within \(7\times10^{-10}\).

Route (c) is NICE on one GPU. It prepares the learned substructure of every cell, solves the assembled system by the preconditioned conjugate gradients of Section 5.8 to a relative residual of \(10^{-6}\) and then computes the sensitivities. In this route, the correction runs in single precision.

All routes include cell setup and stiffness assembly, and none includes the one-off cost of data generation and training. The two exact routes solve six loads but do not evaluate sensitivities, which would add to their time. In route (a), the solution of the six loads takes 3–4% of the total time (Supplementary Table ST12b). Since the routes run on different processors, one GPU against 16 CPU threads, the comparison concerns the routes as each is deployed and does not measure hardware-equivalent amounts of work.

For the two eight-cell lattices, an analysis with NICE takes 79 and 105 s. The whole-lattice direct solution takes 868 and 1,042 s, about ten times as long, and conventional exact static condensation takes longer still (Table 5). On the four-cell lattices, the time ratios are similar, and the compliance computed with NICE agrees with that of the direct solution to within 0.022%. More threads reduce the time of the direct solution only slightly, because cell setup and assembly do not become faster. Supplementary Table ST12b gives the times with 32 threads and with one thread. In route (a), cell setup and stiffness assembly on the CPU take 41 to 53% of the time. In route (c), the cell preparation on the GPU contains setup and assembly as well as the network encoding and the setup of the correction. With these preparation phases excluded from both routes, NICE remains five to eight times faster (Supplementary Tables ST12b and ST12d). For the eight-cell lattices, the timed compliance errors include the algebraic error of the solve to \(10^{-6}\) (Eq. The memory requirements follow the same pattern. For the eight-cell lattices, the peak process memory of the direct solution is 71 and 86 GiB. This peak is set by the Cholesky factor, which grows slightly faster than the number of DOFs. Conventional exact static condensation needs 40 and 42 GiB, most of it for the condensed cell matrices, which are held until their elimination (Supplementary Table ST12). NICE needs at most 9.2 GiB of GPU memory and holds at most 4.7 GiB of CPU memory after cell preparation. Per cell, the stored state of NICE takes 0.25 to 1.37 GiB, whereas the interior Cholesky factor of conventional exact static condensation takes 0.60 to 9.4 GiB (Supplementary Table ST13).

Iterative solvers for the whole lattice were compared on the two eight-cell lattices. They solve the systems of route (a) with PETSc [Balay et al. (2026)](https://petsc.org/release/manual/) on the same CPU with 16 MPI processes (Supplementary Table ST12e). Conjugate gradients preconditioned by smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511) reach a relative residual of \(10^{-4}\) in 52 to 84 s per load, after a setup of 35 and 39 s. At this residual, the compliance differs from that of the converged solution by less than \(5\times10^{-10}\). Including the setup and assembly of route (a), this solver takes 637 and 752 s for the three loads of route (c), eight and seven times as long as NICE. Classical algebraic multigrid (BoomerAMG [Falgout & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-47789-6_66)) gave an indefinite preconditioner for one lattice and needed 250 to 260 iterations per load for the other. For BDDC [Dohrmann (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1137/S1064827502412887) with exact local solvers and one subdomain per cell, the factorisation of the Dirichlet and complete Neumann matrices of every cell alone takes 449 and 523 s (Supplementary Note S3). Every fine-scale route repeats the cell setup and the assembly of the whole-lattice system. In route (a), these two steps take 416 and 474 s (Supplementary Table ST12b), which alone is 5.3 and 4.5 times the time of the NICE analysis.

In a design iteration, the geometry of every cell changes, so the preparation of every cell is repeated, as in the analyses of Table 5. For the conventional routes, this preparation is the factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix; for NICE, it is the network encoding and the setup of the correction. The assembled solve can start from the previous solution. The conventional routes can reuse their symbolic factorisation as long as the pattern of active elements does not change.

### 5.10. Thickness design

Two cases are optimised with NICE. Case A is the \(2\times2\times2\) block of Section 5.8. The second case is a cut plate supported on its cut, which is described below and shown in Figure 13. In both cases, the corner thickness parameters solve the design problem of Section 2.3 under one unit traction load on a face, with \(V^*=0.8\,V(\boldsymbol\tau^0)\). The parameters at the vertices in the plane of the loaded face are fixed; these are 9 of 27 parameters in case A and 10 of 74 in the plate. This keeps the nodal load independent of the design and removes the load-derivative term \(2f_{g,c}^T\widehat U\) of Appendix H. The optimiser receives the vertex gradient of Section 2.3, with the sensitivities \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}_m\) of Section 4.2 in place of \(\boldsymbol s_m\). The bounds are \(0.18\le\tau\le0.69\). The corner span of a cell is the largest difference between two of its corner parameters. It is limited to 0.45 in each cell, and so is the norm of the gradient of \(\tau\). These bounds and limits keep every cell inside the parameter domain of the training data. In this domain, the corner parameters lie in [0.175, 0.699], and the corner span and the gradient norm are at most 0.47 (Section 6.3). Each design iteration regenerates the geometry and the learned substructure of every cell. It then solves the lattice from the previous solution by the preconditioned conjugate gradients of Section 5.8. Finally, it takes one step of the method of moving asymptotes (MMA) [Svanberg (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1620240207), in the variant of Svanberg (2007). The move limit is 5% of the parameter range, and no line search is used (Supplementary Table ST16).

It is optimised from its graded design with NICE and, for comparison, with exact static condensation; the latter is called the twin run (Figure 14a). Both runs stop when the relative change of the objective has stayed below \(10^{-4}\) in three consecutive iterations (Supplementary Table ST16). Their final corner parameters differ by at most 0.0051, and the exact compliances of their final designs differ by \(1.25\times10^{-5}\) of their value. The lattice gradient has errors of at most 0.33%, and every component has the exact sign (Table 3). Both errors grow as the design approaches the bounds (Supplementary Table ST17).

The plate supported on its cut is a single layer of \(8\times4\) cells. A planar cut removes eight of these cells and leaves 16 uncut and eight cut cells (Supplementary Note S6.3). The plate is clamped at its cut: every DOF of the cut-plane elements of every cut cell is fixed. This support lies within one element, of size \(h=1/32\), of the cut surface. Because the DOFs of the cut-plane elements are retained, NICE represents this support without retraining. The end face of the plate is four cells wide and carries a traction load of unit resultant that acts in the plane of the plate and normal to its long side. The plate thus acts as an in-plane cantilever supported along the oblique cut (Figure 15a). The cut normal, with \(\vartheta=33.7^\circ\), lies in the range \(0<\vartheta<\pi/4\) of orientations in which NICE was mainly evaluated (Sections 5.1 and 6.3).

Starting from the uniform design \(\tau=0.40\), NICE stops after 24 design iterations by the rule on the change of the objective (Figure 14b). The compliance rises from 75.59 to 110.49 while the volume is reduced to \(V^*\), and then falls to 78.26. The final design reaches both bounds as well as the corner-span and gradient limits. Its thickest walls lie near the end of the support closest to the loaded face and along the opposite free edge. The cells far from the load are thinned to the lower bound (Figure 15a). Checked with exact static condensation, the NICE compliance lies 0.011% below the exact one at the uniform start and 0.030% below it at the final design. At these two designs, the lattice gradient has errors of 0.029% and 0.30%, and all 64 components have the exact sign (Supplementary Table ST21). Two uniform designs of the same volume serve as references. In the first, the ten parameters of the loaded face are kept at 0.40 and the other 64 take the common value that meets \(V^*\); in the second, all 74 parameters take the same value. Against the first uniform design, the NICE design lowers the exact compliance by 32.1%, from 115.36 to 78.29, and against the second by 30.9%. The homogenisation design described below lowers it by 30.5% against the first. For both uniform designs, the NICE compliance agrees with the exact one to within 0.019%, and the gradient to within 0.064% (Supplementary Table ST21).

For comparison, the plate is also optimised with a homogenised model, as in homogenisation-based graded design [Li et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2018.06.003). The elasticity tensor of this model is that of the cell with uniform thickness, obtained by periodic homogenisation on the same discrete model (Supplementary Table ST18b). The homogenised model is clamped on the plane of the cut. It is optimised with the same variables, bounds, limits, volume bound and optimiser as the NICE run. Its volume is computed from the homogenised material volume fraction \(V^H(\tau)\). At the uniform start, this volume equals the material volume of the discrete model to within \(1.2\times10^{-7}\). The homogenised model underestimates the exact compliance of the uniform start by 27.0%, which is consistent with the absence of scale separation through the thickness of a single layer. With 12 instead of 6 elements per cell and axis, the underestimate is 25.8%. The clamp of the homogenised model is imposed by a penalty, and a hundredfold larger penalty factor changes the macroscale compliance by less than 0.001% (Supplementary Note S6.3). The underestimate is not caused by the support. The fine-scale model clamps all cut-plane elements, a support at least as stiff as one acting on the cut surface alone. This support lowers the exact compliance and therefore narrows the difference. Along its own optimisation path, the macroscale compliance lies 27–33% below the NICE compliance; up to iteration 3, the two runs analyse the same designs (Figure 14b). Analysed on the cut geometry, the homogenisation design uses 0.18% less material than the NICE design, and its compliance is 2.44% higher. This difference is the same whether both designs are evaluated with NICE or with exact static condensation, which gives exact compliances of 80.198 and 78.287 (Supplementary Tables ST18c and ST21). The corner parameters of the homogenisation design differ from those of the NICE design by up to 0.26, with a correlation of 0.95 (Supplementary Table ST18c). Figure 15b shows these corner parameters. The homogenised model thus underestimates the compliance of the cut layer by about a quarter on both macroscale meshes and leads to a design that is less stiff on the cut geometry.

The discrete model of a cell can change from one design to the next (Section 4.2). In both cases, the discrete model changed between every pair of consecutive design iterations. The change affected at least 3 of the 8 cells of case A and at least 11 of the 24 cells of the plate (Supplementary Table ST19b). In these runs, the residual work of Eq. (16) stayed at most \(5.4\times10^{-8}\) of the compliance. In four design iterations over all runs, the geometry generator required a perturbation of at most \(5.8\times10^{-4}\) in a corner parameter (Supplementary Table ST19a). A NICE design iteration took 74 s on average for case A and 200 s for the plate (Table 6). For the plate, single iterations took 155–270 s, and the 24 iterations took 1.3 h in total (Supplementary Table ST18a).

To examine how the cost grows with the size of the lattice, the design iteration was timed on plates with 24, 51, 88 and 110 cells. These plates, with the proportions and the cut of the plate above, are clamped along the uncut long side and loaded in plane on the opposite face (Figure 16). The largest plate has 32.7 million DOFs in the cut finite element model and 1.62 million free retained DOFs. Each plate was run for its first four design iterations. Learned substructures beyond 4 GiB of GPU memory were streamed from CPU memory. The time of a design iteration grew in proportion to the number of cells, at 10.1 to 10.9 s per cell, and reached 19.4 min for 110 cells. The conjugate gradients needed 138 to 173 iterations. The CPU memory, which holds the streamed state of the learned cells, grew by about 0.7 GiB per cell. With the learned cells on the GPU held within a fixed 4 GiB, the GPU memory grew with the assembled retained system. The preconditioner factorises the stiffness matrix \(\mathbb K_{PP}\) of this system directly (Supplementary Table ST20). ## 6. Discussion

### 6.1. Retained degrees of freedom, network and correction

Using the complete set of retained DOFs, without reducing the interface, separates two choices that a reduced substructure model combines: the displacement patterns that neighbouring cells can exchange, and the accuracy with which the interior responds to each of them. The network and the correction affect only the second choice, and the ablation of Section 5.7 shows the role of the first. Even with exact interior displacements, restricting the cell-face displacements changes the assembled response, and a polynomial degree that gives an accurate compliance can still leave an appreciable error in the sensitivity. In the two-cell assemblies of the cut cells examined, restricting the cell-face displacements produced larger errors in the local sensitivity than the interior approximation of NICE (Sections 5.6 and 5.7). This comparison was made at a fixed cell size and without the refinement of the partition or the enrichment that methods with reduced boundary DOFs use. In addition, the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements carry the support of the plate of Section 5.10 directly, whereas the homogenised model needs a separate constraint on the plane of the cut for this support. Where the cut surfaces carry no loads or supports, however, these DOFs enlarge the assembled system (Section 6.3), whose solution takes most of the time of a NICE analysis (Supplementary Table ST12d).

The structure of the network makes it possible to learn the displacement recovery on the complete set of retained DOFs (Section 3.2). Its weights act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels, so one network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves every set of retained DOFs. The same network, unchanged, was used for the 80 validation geometries, the sixteen cells of the lattices and the cut cells of the plate supported on its cut (Sections 5.3, 5.8 and 5.10). Because the network output is exactly linear in the retained displacements, the relations of Sections 3.1 and 4 apply to the network itself (Section 3.2). Because the correction is linear and fixed for each geometry, the network can be trained with the correction applied (Section 3.5). The condensed stiffness is applied without being formed, so each cell stores the quantities determined by its geometry rather than a dense matrix on its retained DOFs (Section 3.6).

Within the interior, the network prediction and the correction are complementary. By the energy formulation of the condensed stiffness, if an interior correction does not increase the \(A\)-energy error for any retained displacements, the corrected condensed stiffness lies between the uncorrected and the exact condensed stiffness, whatever the network (Section 3.4). An interior displacement error of about 1% gives an energy error of 1.8 to 35%, because the error is stiffer than the exact solution (Section 5.4). Chebyshev smoothing removes the error components with large Rayleigh quotients of the Jacobi-scaled interior stiffness matrix. The coarse-grid correction removes the smooth components that smoothing reduces slowly. Under the same correction, the network prediction leaves lower energy errors than zero interior displacements and the graph-harmonic field (Table 4). Even with eight times the smoothing work, the graph-harmonic field leaves a larger error than the eight-step result of the base network in five of six cells (Section 5.5). Conversely, the correction reduces the error components that the network represents poorly. On the moderately cut cell M1, it lowers the energy error of the base network from 13.5% to 0.19% without any change of the network parameters. In the two-cell configurations, Base network + correction already stays below the 3% lines. Training with the correction applied adds less than the correction itself: it reduces the mean energy error by a further factor of 1.31 (Section 5.3).

### 6.2. Computational value

A number of correction steps that suffices for the compliance can leave the local sensitivity inaccurate. The relevant cost is therefore the total work needed to reach the prescribed accuracy in both quantities. On the lattices of Section 5.9, NICE is faster than the whole-lattice direct solution and conventional exact static condensation. On the two eight-cell lattices, on which iterative solvers were compared, it is also faster than whole-lattice conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid. It also needs less memory than the two direct routes. The advantage comes from the way the interior is resolved. The exact routes, direct or iterative, solve the fine-scale problem of every cell or of the whole lattice. NICE obtains the interior displacements of each cell from one network evaluation and a fixed number of correction steps, and it solves the assembled problem on the retained DOFs only (Section 5.9). Inexact BDDC and FETI-DP methods [Li & Widlund (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2006.03.011), [Klawonn & Rheinbach (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1758), [Hirschler et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.7419) use approximate subdomain and coarse solves only inside the preconditioner, and therefore converge to the exact discrete solution. The lattice solve of NICE, in contrast, iterates on the assembled condensed stiffness matrices \(\widehat S\) of the learned substructures, so the approximation lies in the operator itself. Fewer smoothing steps would lower the cost of applying the condensed stiffness, at the loss of accuracy quantified in Section 5.5.

### 6.3. Limitations

The trained network applies to the setting in which it was trained and tested. This setting consists of Schwarz-P-type cells of Eq. (1) with eight corner parameters in [0.175, 0.699] and with corner span and gradient norm of at most 0.47 (Supplementary Table ST02). Each cell has at most one planar cut, whose normal is the image of \((\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta,0)\) under a symmetry of the cube. The network is also tied to one discretisation, with \(n=32\), \(E_Y=1\), \(\nu=0.3\) and \(\gamma=10^{-4}\). Its grid hierarchy, with 65, 33, 17 and 9 positions per axis, is matched to this discretisation. Other TPMS families need further training. On gyroid sheet cells, the network trained on Schwarz-P cells gives mean energy errors under loads and supports that are 56 to 234 times those on the corresponding Schwarz-P cells. A further 8,000 training steps with eighteen gyroid cells lower these errors six- to tenfold, but they remain 6 to 23 times the Schwarz-P level (Supplementary Note S7). Other families therefore require new training data and network parameters, as do cells with several cuts or curved boundaries, other resolutions and other materials. Training on several families and testing on a family held out would show how far the network itself transfers; this is left to future work. The network is not equivariant under the symmetries of the cube: for a rotated or reflected cell, its prediction is in general not the correspondingly rotated or reflected prediction. Symmetry-decoupled shape functions for voxel substructures have this property [Jiang, C. et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compstruct.2026.120865). NICE was evaluated mainly in the identity orientation. It was also evaluated on the 80 validation geometries in a second orientation, obtained by a symmetry of the cube. This orientation was also used in checkpoint selection. In this orientation, its mean energy error over the seven load classes evaluated in both orientations is 4% higher in relative terms, and under traction loads it is 12% higher (Supplementary Table ST03c).

The properties of the condensed stiffness shown in Section 3.1 hold for any input to the network, but the accuracy does not. No certified error bound is provided (Appendix G.4). Because \(\widehat S\succeq S\), the learned model is stiffer than the reference model and underestimates the compliance (Eq. (15)). Its compliance is therefore not a conservative estimate. The interior residual of Eq. (7) is available at run time, but the energy error that it determines requires one interior solve per test displacement. The lower bound of Appendix I needs no reference solution and can detect a large error for a test displacement, but it cannot confirm a small one. Accuracy is measured against the discrete reference model (Section 5.2). At \(n=32\), this model deviates from its refinement to \(n=64\) by at most 0.28% in the compliance and 0.44% in the sensitivity. The exception is the heavily cut cell H2, where the deviations reach about 1% and 3.6% (Supplementary Table ST10).

The evidence on assembled models consists of the following examples. The two-cell assemblies comprise fourteen configurations of cells used for checkpoint selection and eighteen configurations of cells outside checkpoint selection (Section 5.6). The lattices are two eight-cell lattices and the two four-cell layers of the block (Sections 5.8 and 5.9). The designs checked against exact static condensation are three designs of the block in case A and five designs of the cut 24-cell plate. They also include the first designs of the 24- and 51-cell plates of the scale study (Section 5.10). All comparisons between trained variants use a single random seed. The effect of the sensitivity term in the loss of Eq. (13) was not isolated. The reported sensitivities are \(\widetilde s_c=-\widehat u^TK_{,c}\widehat u\). They omit the design dependence of the recovery operator \(F\), which enters the derivative of Eq. (18). A central-difference check of the approximate compliance cannot decide whether \(\widehat C_{,c}\) or \(\widetilde s_c\) is the more accurate gradient. At least one of the discrete choices listed in Appendix B.1 switches in 21 to 48 of the 49 rebuilds of each lattice cell. In addition, the difference quotient grows as the step decreases (Supplementary Note S4.3). The sensitivity \(\widetilde s_c\) needs no difference quotient. For the two eight-cell lattices and the two four-cell layers, it gives the gradient with respect to the shared parameters to within 0.02–0.13% under the face loads and 0.22–0.35% under the random loads (Supplementary Table ST14). The optimisations of Section 5.10 take steps without a line search. A line search would compare the approximate compliance with \(\widetilde s_c\), which is not its derivative. Where the cut surfaces carry no loads or supports, the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements enlarge the assembled system. In the lattices of Sections 5.8 and 5.9, the DOFs of the cut-plane elements that do not lie on the cell faces make up 37 to 50% of the free retained DOFs. A reduced representation of these DOFs where the cut carries no loads or supports is a natural next step. On one GPU, the size of the lattice that can be analysed is at present limited by the direct factorisation of \(\mathbb K_{PP}\) in the preconditioner. The memory of this factorisation grows with the assembled retained system, whereas the memory of the learned cells stays within a fixed budget. A further level in the preconditioner or an iterative coarse solve is the natural way to remove this limit.

Fine-scale iterative solvers of the whole lattice were compared on the two eight-cell lattices only. The comparison used algebraic multigrid [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511), [Henson & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0168-9274(01)00115-5), [Falgout & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-47789-6_66) in standard settings, and the local factorisation cost of BDDC with exact local solvers [Dohrmann (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1137/S1064827502412887) (Section 5.9). FETI-DP [Farhat et al. (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.76) and tuned multigrid were not compared. An optimiser must keep the designs inside the parameter domain stated above, as the bounds and limits of Section 5.10 do. The optimisations of that section are local.

## 7. Conclusions

NICE is a learned component model that keeps the properties of static condensation on the complete set of retained DOFs of a cut cell. This set changes with the cell geometry. A network predicts the interior displacements, and a fixed two-grid correction reduces the interior force imbalance that the network leaves. The condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy of the corrected field and applied with the transpose of the complete recovery operator, so it is symmetric positive semidefinite, and only the rigid-body modes have zero energy. It is never softer than the exact condensed stiffness, and its error is quadratic in the interior displacement error (Proposition 1). These properties do not depend on how well the network is trained. A correction that satisfies the conditions of Proposition 2 cannot increase the error of the condensed stiffness. In every example of Supplementary Table ST07, a larger number of smoothing steps reduced the error further. The network and the correction complement each other. Under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field leaves 5.4 to 265 times the error of NICE. Applied to the base network without retraining, the correction alone lowers the mean energy error under traction loads on the 80 validation geometries from 6.89% to 0.0965%.

The error relations of Section 4 determine the quantities on which such a model must be checked. The interior error enters the assembled compliance weighted by the energy fraction of each cell (Proposition 3) and the thickness sensitivity through a term linear in that error (Proposition 4). In cut cells, the displacement errors of the base network are about 1%, but they are stiffer than the exact solution and give energy errors of 1.8 to 35% (Section 5.4). These relations hold for any approximate recovery operator, so an approximate static condensation used in design must be checked on its sensitivities as well as on its compliance. NICE is trained and checked on both.

For cut thin-walled Schwarz-P cells with tens of thousands of retained DOFs, the mean energy error of NICE over the 80 validation geometries is 0.074%. In every two-cell configuration, its compliance and sensitivity errors are at most 0.27% and 1.49%. An analysis of an eight-cell lattice with sensitivities takes 79–105 s on one GPU. On 16 CPU threads or processes, the whole-lattice direct solution takes 868–1,042 s, and whole-lattice conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid take 637–752 s. Thickness optimisation with NICE reproduces the design that exact static condensation gives for an eight-cell lattice, to within 0.0051 in every corner parameter. For a 24-cell plate clamped at its cut, a homogenised model underestimates the compliance by 27%. For the same plate, the compliance and the gradient from NICE differ from those of exact static condensation by at most 0.032% and 0.32% at the five checked designs. The optimised design has a 32% lower compliance than the uniform design of the same volume. On the plates of the scale study, exact checks up to 51 cells give compliance errors below 0.014% and gradient errors below 0.05%. A design iteration of a 110-cell plate with 32.7 million DOFs takes 19 min.

Homogenisation loses accuracy where the scales do not separate, and a fine-scale model of every wall must be solved again at every design iteration. Between these two approaches, NICE provides a component model that keeps the properties of static condensation without factorising the interior stiffness matrix of each cell. Including the coarse-grid correction in the network, as a coarse level that solves the coarse-grid problem with the matrix \(V^TAV\), would leave the smoothing as the only fixed stage. The properties of the condensed stiffness follow from its computation through the strain energy and do not depend on the network. The error relations and the correction therefore apply to any family of components with a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix and a positive definite interior block. For a new family of cells, the network has to be retrained, whereas the error relations and the correction carry over unchanged.
