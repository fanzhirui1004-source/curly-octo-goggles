## 1. Introduction

The stiffness of a lattice can be tailored by grading the thickness of its cells [Yu et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matdes.2019.108021). When a lattice trimmed to the shape of a part is designed in this way, the thickness parameters of the cells are the design variables, and their distribution is determined by iterative optimisation. The design depends on local responses, such as the stiffness of a cut cell next to a support and the sensitivity of the compliance to the thickness of a cell, so the analysis must resolve the geometry of every cell. At the same time, the whole structure is reanalysed at every design iteration, and the cost of each analysis, which grows with the size of the model, accumulates over the optimisation. Accuracy thus calls for a model that retains the detail of every cell, whereas efficiency calls for a model that is as small as possible.

Fine-scale finite element models discretise every cell and meet the accuracy requirement; with large-scale parallel solvers, such models have been used in topology optimisation at very large scale [Aage et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23911). Because every design iteration changes the geometry of every cell, however, a fine-scale model must be reassembled and solved again at every iteration (Section 5.9). Homogenisation replaces the cells by an equivalent material; it is inexpensive and is the usual basis for the design of graded lattices [Li et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2018.06.003), [Wu et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00158-021-02881-8). It assumes periodic boundary conditions on a unit cell, an assumption that fails without scale separation or when cells are cut: near supports, loads and the cut surface, the displacement field varies on the scale of a cell, and a cut cell is no longer a complete periodic cell. For the plate of Section 5.10, a single layer of cells clamped at its cut (Figure 1), the homogenised model underestimates the compliance of the uniform starting design by 27%.

Substructuring lies between these two approaches. Each cell is treated as a substructure, or superelement, whose interior degrees of freedom are eliminated by static condensation, so that only the retained (master) degrees of freedom through which the cell connects to its neighbours, supports and loads remain [Guyan (1965)](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2874), [Irons (1965)](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3027). The global system is assembled and solved on the retained degrees of freedom only, and the interior displacements of each cell are recovered afterwards to evaluate local quantities such as the sensitivities. Static condensation introduces no approximation, but it requires a factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix for every cell geometry. For cut thin-walled cells, the retained degrees of freedom are those of all nodes on the cell faces and of all nodes of the elements intersected by the trimming plane, hereafter referred to as cut-plane elements; for the 80 validation geometries of this paper, a cell has 2,679 to 45,900 retained degrees of freedom, with a median of about \(2.4\times10^4\). Because every design iteration changes the geometry of every cell, these factorisations must be repeated at every iteration.

Learned component models replace the interior solution of a substructure by a neural network. One class of models maps nodal displacements directly to nodal forces [Capuano & Rimoli (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2018.10.046); learned substructures [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041), [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955) predict numerical shape functions with a neural network, compute the substructure stiffness matrix as \(N^TKN\) and assemble the substructures in the manner of finite elements. To keep the network output small, these methods describe the boundary displacement of each substructure by a few degrees of freedom, for example through linear or cubic Bézier interpolation, and the network outputs one shape function for each boundary degree of freedom; port reduction [Eftang & Patera (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4543), [Smetana & Patera (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1137/15m1009603), [Ballani et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2017.09.014) likewise reduces the degrees of freedom shared between components. Reducing the boundary degrees of freedom restricts the displacements that neighbouring substructures can exchange, and the resulting model error cannot be removed by a more accurate interior approximation. For cut cells, supports and loads on the cut surface act on all nodes of the cut-plane elements, whose degrees of freedom must therefore be retained; their number is large and varies with the cell geometry, so that one network output per retained degree of freedom is impractical.

This paper therefore proposes neural-initialised static condensation with equilibrium correction (NICE). NICE retains all degrees of freedom on the cell faces and in the cut-plane elements and approximates only the interior displacements: a neural network that takes the cell geometry as input provides an initial interior displacement field, and one two-grid cycle of Chebyshev smoothing and coarse-grid correction [Xu (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1137/1034116), applied with the retained displacements held fixed, reduces the interior force imbalance. The network is nonlinear in the geometry but exactly linear in the retained displacements. Its parameters act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels rather than on individual degrees of freedom, so that one network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves cells with different numbers of retained degrees of freedom. The smoothing mainly reduces the high-frequency part of the interior error, and the coarse-grid correction reduces its smooth part. The network and the correction together define a linear map from the retained displacements to the displacements of the whole cell; the condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy of the resulting displacement field, and neither the map nor the condensed stiffness is formed as a matrix.

Because the condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy, it is symmetric positive semidefinite, and its only zero-energy modes are rigid-body motions. By the principle of minimum potential energy, it is an upper bound on the exact condensed stiffness, with an error of second order in the interior displacement error, and it reduces to exact static condensation when the interior displacements are exact. These properties require only that the interior approximation be linear in the retained displacements and reproduce them and rigid-body motions, and therefore do not depend on the training accuracy of the network. Since the two-grid correction is a fixed linear operation, the corrected condensed stiffness retains these properties; when the smoothing interval and the coarse solve satisfy the conditions of Proposition 2, the correction does not increase the error [Golub & Varga (1961)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01386013), [Xu & Zikatanov (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0894-0347-02-00398-3). The network is trained with the correction applied, and the two complement each other: under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field, which has no trainable parameters, leaves 5.4 to 265 times the mean energy error of NICE (Section 5.5).

The interior displacement error affects the compliance and the sensitivities differently. The compliance error is a sum of cell errors weighted by the fractions of the strain energy that the cells carry, and is of second order in the interior displacement error. The thickness sensitivities, by contrast, contain a term linear in this error, introduced through the derivative of the stiffness matrix with respect to the thickness, which the energy error bounds only at the order of its square root. A cell that carries a small fraction of the energy therefore has little effect on the compliance but may still have inaccurate sensitivities, and for any approximate static condensation used in design, the accuracy of the compliance does not ensure that of the sensitivities. Similar observations have been made for approximate reanalysis and inexact solves in topology optimisation [Amir et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.2536), [Amir et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00158-009-0463-4), [Gogu (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4797). Section 4 derives these relations for any interior approximation that meets the conditions above (Propositions 3 and 4); accordingly, NICE is trained and checked on both the compliance and the sensitivities (Section 5.6).

The main contributions are as follows.

- All degrees of freedom on the faces of a cut cell and in its cut-plane elements are retained, and only the interior displacements are approximated. Although these degrees of freedom change with the geometry, supports and loads on the cut surface act on the original degrees of freedom and can be applied without retraining (Sections 2, 5.7 and 5.10).
- A neural network for the interior displacements is constructed whose output is, by construction, exactly linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them and rigid-body motions; its size does not depend on the number of retained degrees of freedom, and it forms no matrix. The condensed stiffness computed from its strain energy is symmetric positive semidefinite and an upper bound on the exact condensed stiffness, with an error of second order in the interior displacement error (Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.6).
- A fixed two-grid correction is introduced into the condensation and reduces the error of a trained network without retraining. It preserves these properties for any discrete model with a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix and a positive definite interior block, and under the conditions of Proposition 2 it does not increase the energy error (Sections 3.3 and 3.4); in every example of Supplementary Table ST07, a larger number of smoothing steps reduced the energy error further (Section 5.5).
- The different routes by which the interior displacement error enters the compliance and the sensitivities are derived. Both quantities are checked accordingly, and the thickness designs are verified against exact static condensation and compared with homogenisation (Sections 4, 5.6, 5.8 and 5.10).

Section 1.1 reviews related work. Section 2 describes the finite element model of the cut cells, static condensation, the design problem and the assumptions of the analysis. Section 3 presents NICE: the computation of the condensed stiffness, the network that predicts the interior displacements, the two-grid correction, the training and the application of the condensed stiffness without forming it. Section 4 analyses the effect of the interior displacement error on the compliance and the sensitivities; the proofs are given in the appendices. Section 5 presents numerical examples for cut thin-walled Schwarz-P cells in a stabilised cut finite element model: the accuracy on 80 validation geometries and on two-cell assemblies, an ablation that restricts the cell-face displacements, eight-cell lattices of cells used neither in training nor in checkpoint selection, the computational cost compared with a direct solve of the whole lattice and with conventional exact static condensation, and thickness optimisation checked against exact static condensation and compared with a homogenised model. Section 6 discusses the scope and limitations of the method, and Section 7 concludes.
