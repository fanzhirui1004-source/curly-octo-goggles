# NICE: neural-initialised static condensation with equilibrium correction, with application to the thickness design of cut TPMS lattices

## Abstract

Thickness optimisation of lattices trimmed to a part's shape needs fine-scale accuracy and fast analysis at every design iteration. Fine-scale finite element models discretise every cell, so each iteration reassembles and solves a large system. Homogenisation assumes periodic boundary conditions, which fail without scale separation or when cells are cut. Static condensation eliminates each cell's interior degrees of freedom, reducing the global system without loss of accuracy, but must refactorise every interior stiffness matrix at each iteration. The proposed method, NICE, avoids these factorisations. It retains all degrees of freedom on cell faces and in elements cut by the trimming plane; a network predicts the interior displacements from the cell geometry, a two-grid correction reduces the interior force imbalance, and the condensed stiffness follows from the corrected field's strain energy. This condensed stiffness bounds the exact one from above, with an error of second order in the interior displacement error, independent of network accuracy. Thickness sensitivities have an error of first order in the interior displacement error, so accurate compliance does not ensure accurate sensitivities; the network is trained and checked on both. For cut Schwarz-P cells, the mean energy error over 80 validation geometries is 0.074%, an eight-cell analysis with sensitivities is about ten times faster than a direct full-lattice solve, and thickness optimisation reproduces the exact-condensation design of an eight-cell lattice. For a 24-cell layer clamped at its cut, where homogenisation underestimates compliance by 27%, NICE agrees with exact condensation to 0.03% in compliance and 0.3% in gradient.

**Keywords:** superelement; static condensation; neural network; multigrid; cut finite element method; TPMS lattice; thickness optimisation.

## 1. Introduction

The stiffness of a lattice can be tailored by grading the thickness of its cells [Yu et al. (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.matdes.2019.108021). In the thickness design of a graded lattice, the thickness parameters of the cells are the design variables, and their distribution is determined by iterative optimisation. The design depends on local responses, such as the stiffness of the cells next to supports and loads and the sensitivity of the compliance to the thickness of each cell, so the response of every cell must be computed accurately. At the same time, the whole structure is reanalysed at every design iteration, and the cost of each analysis, which grows with the size of the model, accumulates over the optimisation. Accuracy thus calls for a model that resolves the deformation at the scale of the cells, whereas efficiency calls for a model that is as small as possible.

Fine-scale finite element models discretise every cell and meet the accuracy requirement; with large-scale parallel solvers, such models have been used in topology optimisation at very large scale [Aage et al. (2017)](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature23911). Because every design iteration changes the geometry of every cell, however, a fine-scale model must be reassembled and solved again at every iteration (Section 5.9). Homogenisation replaces the cells by an equivalent material; it is inexpensive and is the usual basis for the design of graded lattices [Li et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2018.06.003), [Wu et al. (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00158-021-02881-8). It imposes periodic boundary conditions on a unit cell, which presumes that the structure is much larger than a cell and that the cells are complete and periodically arranged. Near supports, loads and boundaries, the displacement field varies on the scale of a cell, so scale separation no longer holds; when a lattice is trimmed to the shape of a part, the cut cells at its boundary are incomplete, so periodicity no longer holds either. For the plate of Section 5.10, a single layer of cells clamped at its cut (Figure 1), the homogenised model underestimates the compliance of the uniform starting design by 27%.

Substructuring lies between these two approaches. Each cell is treated as a substructure, or superelement, whose interior degrees of freedom are eliminated by static condensation, so that only the retained (master) degrees of freedom through which the cell connects to its neighbours, supports and loads remain [Guyan (1965)](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.2874), [Irons (1965)](https://doi.org/10.2514/3.3027). The global system is assembled and solved on the retained degrees of freedom only, and the interior displacements of each cell are recovered afterwards to evaluate local quantities such as the sensitivities. For cut thin-walled cells, the retained degrees of freedom are those of all nodes on the cell faces and of all nodes of the elements intersected by the trimming plane, hereafter referred to as cut-plane elements; for the 80 validation geometries of this paper, a cell has 2,679 to 45,900 retained degrees of freedom, with a median of about \(2.4\times10^4\), and several times as many interior ones (Figure 1b). Static condensation introduces no approximation, but it requires a sparse Cholesky factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix of every cell, from which a dense condensed stiffness matrix is formed. For a cell with the median number of retained degrees of freedom, this matrix occupies 4.2 GiB in double precision, and for the deployed cells of Section 5.9 the Cholesky factor of a single cell occupies 0.60–9.4 GiB. Because every design iteration changes the geometry of every cell, these factorisations must be repeated for all cells at every iteration; on the lattices of Section 5.9, one analysis by conventional exact static condensation takes longer than a direct solve of the whole lattice.

Learned component models obtain the stiffness relation of an element or a substructure from a neural network, so that no interior equilibrium problem of the component has to be solved during the analysis. For example, [Capuano & Rimoli (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2018.10.046) map the nodal displacements of an element directly to its nodal forces, and learned substructures [Huang et al. (2023)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.eml.2023.102041), [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955) predict numerical shape functions with a neural network, compute the substructure stiffness matrix as \(N^TKN\) and assemble the substructures in the manner of finite elements. To keep the network output small, learned substructures describe the boundary displacement of each substructure by a few degrees of freedom, for example through linear or cubic Bézier interpolation, and the network outputs one shape function for each boundary degree of freedom; port reduction [Eftang & Patera (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4543), [Smetana & Patera (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1137/15m1009603), [Ballani et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2017.09.014) likewise reduces the degrees of freedom shared between components. Reducing the boundary degrees of freedom restricts the displacements that neighbouring substructures can exchange, and the resulting model error cannot be removed by a more accurate interior approximation. For cut cells, the supports and loads on the cut surface act on all nodes of the cut-plane elements, whose degrees of freedom must therefore all be retained, so a description of the boundary displacement by a few degrees of freedom is unsuitable. Since the retained degrees of freedom are numerous and vary with the cell geometry, one network output per retained degree of freedom is also impractical.

This paper therefore proposes neural-initialised static condensation with equilibrium correction (NICE). NICE retains all degrees of freedom on the cell faces and in the cut-plane elements and approximates only the interior displacements: a neural network that takes the cell geometry as input provides an initial interior displacement field, and one two-grid cycle of Chebyshev smoothing and coarse-grid correction [Xu (1992)](https://doi.org/10.1137/1034116), applied with the retained displacements held fixed, reduces the interior force imbalance. The network is nonlinear in the geometry but exactly linear in the retained displacements. Its parameters act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels rather than on individual degrees of freedom, so that one network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves cells with different numbers of retained degrees of freedom. The smoothing mainly reduces the high-frequency part of the interior error, and the coarse-grid correction reduces its smooth part. The network and the correction together define a linear map from the retained displacements to the displacements of the whole cell, and the condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy of the resulting displacement field. The assembled system is solved by preconditioned conjugate gradients, which need only products of the condensed stiffness with vectors; each product is obtained by applying the network, the correction, the cell stiffness matrix and the transposes of the correction and the network in turn, so neither the map nor the condensed stiffness has to be formed.

The output of the NICE network is exactly linear in the retained displacements and, by construction, reproduces them and rigid-body motions, so the condensed stiffness computed from the strain energy of the resulting displacement field is symmetric positive semidefinite, with rigid-body motions as its only zero-energy modes. By the principle of minimum potential energy, it is an upper bound on the exact condensed stiffness, with an error of second order in the interior displacement error; these properties follow from the network architecture and do not depend on the training accuracy. The two-grid correction is a fixed linear operation that leaves the retained displacements and rigid-body motions unchanged, so the corrected condensed stiffness retains these properties; when the smoothing interval and the coarse solve satisfy the conditions of Proposition 2, the correction does not increase the error [Golub & Varga (1961)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01386013), [Xu & Zikatanov (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1090/S0894-0347-02-00398-3). The network is trained with the correction applied, and the two complement each other: under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field, which has no trainable parameters, leaves 5.4 to 265 times the mean energy error of NICE (Section 5.5).

The interior displacement error affects the compliance and the sensitivities differently. The compliance error is a sum of cell errors weighted by the fractions of the strain energy that the cells carry, and is of second order in the interior displacement error. The thickness sensitivities, by contrast, contain a term of first order in this error, introduced through the derivative of the stiffness matrix with respect to the thickness, which the energy error bounds only at the order of its square root. An accurate compliance therefore does not ensure accurate sensitivities: a cell that carries a small fraction of the energy has little effect on the compliance but may still have inaccurate sensitivities. Similar observations have been made for approximate reanalysis and inexact solves in topology optimisation [Amir et al. (2009)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.2536), [Amir et al. (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00158-009-0463-4), [Gogu (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.4797). Section 4 derives these relations for any interior approximation with the linearity and reproduction properties above (Propositions 3 and 4); accordingly, NICE is trained and checked on both the compliance and the sensitivities (Section 5.6).

The main contributions are as follows.

- All degrees of freedom on the faces of a cut cell and in its cut-plane elements are retained, and only the interior displacements are approximated. Although these degrees of freedom change with the geometry, supports and loads on the cut surface act on the original degrees of freedom and can be applied without retraining (Sections 2, 5.7 and 5.10).
- A network architecture is proposed for sets of retained degrees of freedom that change with the cut: its parameters act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels, so its size does not depend on the number of retained degrees of freedom, and its output is exactly linear in the retained displacements and reproduces them and rigid-body motions. The energy properties of static condensation therefore carry over to the learned model: the condensed stiffness is symmetric positive semidefinite and an upper bound on the exact condensed stiffness, with an error of second order in the interior displacement error, independent of the training accuracy (Sections 3.1, 3.2 and 3.6).
- A fixed two-grid correction is introduced into the condensation and reduces the error of a trained network without retraining. It preserves these properties for any discrete model with a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix and a positive definite interior block, and under the conditions of Proposition 2 it does not increase the energy error (Sections 3.3 and 3.4); in every example of Supplementary Table ST07, a larger number of smoothing steps reduced the energy error further (Section 5.5).
- The different routes by which the interior displacement error enters the compliance and the sensitivities are derived. Both quantities are checked accordingly, and the thickness designs are verified against exact static condensation and compared with homogenisation (Sections 4, 5.6, 5.8 and 5.10).

Section 3 presents NICE: the computation of the condensed stiffness, the network that predicts the interior displacements, the two-grid correction, the training and the application of the condensed stiffness without forming it. Section 4 analyses the effect of the interior displacement error on the compliance and the sensitivities; the proofs are given in the appendices. Section 5 presents numerical examples for cut thin-walled Schwarz-P cells in a stabilised cut finite element model: the accuracy on 80 validation geometries and on two-cell assemblies, an ablation that restricts the cell-face displacements, eight-cell lattices of cells used neither in training nor in checkpoint selection, the computational cost compared with a direct solve of the whole lattice and with conventional exact static condensation, and thickness optimisation checked against exact static condensation and compared with a homogenised model. Section 6 discusses the scope and limitations of the method, and Section 7 concludes.
