### 5.3. Accuracy on single cells

In every cut-severity group, the mean energy error of NICE is about 0.1% or less (Figure 5), and under traction loads its group means are lower than those of the base network by factors of 71 to 117. From uncut to heavily cut cells, the group means of NICE rise from 0.016% to 0.109%, about sevenfold, whereas those of the base network rise from 1.14% to 12.7% (Supplementary Table ST03b). Under traction loads, the mean energy errors of NICE and the base network over all 80 geometries are 0.074% and 6.89%, and their largest single-geometry means are 0.65% and 48.6%, respectively. Over the 5,120 sampled test displacements under traction loads, 99% of the energy errors of NICE lie below 0.69%, and the largest is 1.24% (Supplementary Table ST03).

Under nodal point loads, the mean energy error of the base network rises from 0.908% for uncut to 11.3% for heavily cut cells, with a largest single-geometry mean of 70.1%, whereas the group means of NICE stay between 0.013% and 0.090%. Under the other load classes, the means of NICE are of the same order (Supplementary Table ST03), and Figure S02 shows the distributions over the geometries. On the 60 geometries outside checkpoint selection, the class means of NICE differ from those over all 80 geometries by at most 0.005 percentage points. Under traction loads, the mean of NICE for the heavily cut group is higher on these 60 geometries, 0.126% against 0.109%, and the five largest single-geometry means of NICE all belong to these 60 geometries (Supplementary Table ST03).

Most of the improvement comes from the correction itself. Applying the correction to the base network at deployment, without retraining, lowers the mean under traction loads from 6.89% to 0.0965%, with a largest single-geometry mean of 0.91%, whereas continuing the training of the base network without the correction leaves its mean nearly unchanged (Supplementary Note S8). NICE continues the training with the correction inside the training loop and draws its training geometries from a larger set; this lowers the mean by a further factor of 1.31, with a 95% bootstrap interval over the geometries of 1.20–1.40 (Supplementary Table ST03). Under the same correction, a deterministic graph-harmonic initial field leaves 5.4 to 265 times the error of NICE (Section 5.5).

### 5.4. Where the network error lies

Part of the error left by the network lies in modes that smoothing damps slowly, and part lies next to the cut. Relative to its amplitude, the error also carries far more strain energy than the exact solution and is in this sense stiffer. This section examines the error before the correction: its distribution over the interior spectrum and in space, the reason why an interior displacement error of about 1% produces an energy error of several percent, and the split of its effect on the sensitivity between the linear and the quadratic term of Eq. (17).

The error of the base network occupies different parts of the Jacobi-scaled interior spectrum in different cells (Figure 6). In M1 under traction loads, the lowest 200 modes contain 24.5% of the error energy but only 4.87% of the energy of the exact field (Supplementary Table ST05b), and all 200 lie below the lower endpoint \(a=0.173\) of the smoothing interval, with \(a=b/30\) (Table 1). In H2, only 66 of the lowest 200 modes lie below \(a=0.138\). Smoothing damps these modes slowly and leaves them to the coarse-grid correction (Section 5.5).

Part of the error of the base network lies next to the cut plane (Figure 7). In M1, the elements within two element widths of the cut plane make up 13% of the elements. For the traction-load test displacement plotted, the exact field places 8% of its energy in these elements, whereas over the four test displacements evaluated, 39–43% of the error energy of the base network lies in this layer. The layer adjoins the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements, whose values the network must propagate into the interior. Relative to the base network, NICE reduces the total error energy by about two orders of magnitude, by factors of 113 to 138, and the element error energies by factors of roughly 25 to 300 (10th to 90th percentiles). NICE also halves the fraction of the error energy in this layer, to 19–22%.

A small displacement error produces a large energy error when the error is stiff. To measure this amplification, let \(d_I\) be the interior displacement error, \(u_I\) the exact interior displacements and \(\delta^2=d_I^TDd_I/u_I^TDu_I\) the Jacobi-weighted relative displacement error. With \(\kappa=(d_I^TAd_I/d_I^TDd_I)/(u^TKu/u_I^TDu_I)\), the ratio of the Rayleigh quotients of the interior error and of the exact field, \(\varepsilon=\delta^2\kappa\) holds identically (Appendix B.3). Under traction loads, the base network has mean \(\delta\) of 0.7–1.3% in M1, M2, H1 and H2, but mean \(\kappa\) of 124 to 7089 (Supplementary Table ST04). The displacement error is therefore small, but relative to its amplitude it is far stiffer than the exact field, and the resulting energy errors are 1.8–35%. In the uncut cell U2, a similar \(\delta\) of 0.74% combines with \(\kappa=85\) and gives an energy error of 0.40%. For NICE, \(\delta\) is 0.05–0.19% and \(\kappa\) is 33 to 582 in the cut cells, and the two values are 0.11% and 47 in U2.

Where the errors of the base network are large, the quadratic term of Eq. (17) dominates the sensitivity error. In M1 and H2 under traction loads, the sensitivity errors at fixed retained displacements are 14.1% and 75.1%, and the linear term contributes only 7.2% and 0.79%, respectively, of the sum of the norms of the linear and quadratic terms. Under traction loads, the linear term contributes 28–43% in the three cells whose energy errors are below 2%, U1, U2 and H1, and under nodal point loads it contributes 24–72% in all six cells. Because the linear term is first order in the displacement error, it dominates as this error tends to zero (Supplementary Table ST05 and Figure S03).

### 5.5. Correction of a fixed network

With the base network held fixed, the effect of the correction can be examined in isolation (Figure 8). Eight Chebyshev smoothing steps at fixed retained displacements reduce the mean energy error in every cell, but unevenly (Supplementary Table ST05a). Under traction loads, the error of H2 falls from 35.0% to 0.205%, whereas that of M1 falls only from 13.5% to 4.69%, and to 2.95% after 32 steps, because much of it lies in modes below the smoothing interval (Section 5.4). The sensitivity error of M1 falls from 14.1% to 2.76%. In U1, the energy error decreases at every step, whereas the sensitivity error varies non-monotonically with the number of steps (Figure 8a,b).

A trilinear coarse-grid correction applied before the same eight steps reduces the error of M1 to 0.230%, and eight additional pre-smoothing steps reduce it further to 0.186%; the coarse space of M1 has 5,601 DOFs for 165,927 interior DOFs. With two, four and eight steps per smoothing stage, the two-grid cycle gives 1.02%, 0.422% and 0.186% on M1, and the eight-step cycle gives about 0.027% on M2 and U1 (Supplementary Table ST07). Supplementary Table ST06 compares other coarse spaces.

Table 4 applies the same correction, at the same retained displacements, to four initial fields: zero interior displacements, a graph-harmonic initial field, the field of the base network and the field of NICE. The graph-harmonic field is computed with a volume-weighted graph Laplacian on the element connectivity, after the same exact separation of the rigid-body part as for the network. After the correction, the energy errors of the graph-harmonic field are 7 to 290 times those of the base network, and those of zero interior displacements 2,400 to 12,000 times (Table 4). Even with 64 steps per stage, eight times the smoothing work, the graph-harmonic field remains 2.6 to 50 times above the eight-step result of the base network under traction loads in five of the six cells of Supplementary Table ST07, which are the cells of Table 4 and U1. Only in H2 does it reach the result of the base network.
