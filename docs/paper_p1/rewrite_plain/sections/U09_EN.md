### 5.6. Two-cell assemblies: compliance and sensitivity

This section checks whether the small single-cell energy errors of Sections 5.3–5.5 give an accurate compliance and accurate local sensitivities after assembly. Each two-cell assembly joins a learned test cell to an exact, uncut neighbouring cell, and the wall thickness is continuous across their interface (Figure 9). The seven test cells, U1, U2, L1, M1, M2, H1 and H3, belong to the geometries used for checkpoint selection (Section 5.1). Each test cell is assembled in two configurations, x and y, and 'M1/x' denotes test cell M1 in configuration x. Each configuration has six face loads. These are traction loads in the three Cartesian directions, applied separately on the loaded face of the test cell and on that of the neighbouring cell. For each load, the compliance and the thickness sensitivity of each cell, a vector of eight components, are compared with their exact values. The nodal load is held at its value for the base design, and Supplementary Note S4.2 gives the load normalisation and the solver used for the pairs. A common reference tolerance of 3% is used for the compliance and for the sensitivity vector of each cell, and it is drawn as the 3% line in the figures. The reported errors are maxima over the six loads and, for the sensitivity, also over both cells. Three traction loads on the cut surface of a test cell are considered separately.

Figure 10 compares Base network + correction and NICE in these configurations. NICE stays below both 3% lines in all fourteen configurations of the seven test cells. Its largest compliance error over the six face loads is 0.056%, on M1/x. Its largest sensitivity error over both cells is 0.67%, on U1/y. The directions of the thickness-sensitivity vectors are reproduced more closely than their magnitudes. In these fourteen configurations, the cosine between each sensitivity vector of NICE and the exact vector is at least 0.999998 (Supplementary Note S4.3).

Nine further validation cells, which were not used for checkpoint selection, were assembled in the same way. Five of them are the geometries with the largest single-cell errors of NICE. The other four were chosen at random, one from each of the uncut, lightly cut, moderately cut and heavily cut groups. All eighteen configurations stay below both 3% lines. The largest errors are 0.27% in the compliance and 1.49% in the sensitivity, both on the geometry with the largest single-cell error (Supplementary Table ST08b). All compliance errors above 0.02% occur on the five geometries with the largest single-cell errors. On the four randomly chosen cells, the compliance errors stay below 0.011% and the sensitivity errors below 0.22%.

Base network + correction also stays below both 3% lines, with a largest sensitivity error of 0.95% on U1/x. Without the correction, the base network exceeds the 3% sensitivity line on U1 and M1, with sensitivity errors up to 12.2%. On M1/x, it also exceeds the 3% compliance line, with a compliance error of 4.38% (Supplementary Table ST08). Continuing the training of the base network without the correction, or with smoothing alone, also leaves sensitivity errors above the 3% line on U1 and M1 (Supplementary Note S8). The correction therefore brings the assembled responses below the 3% lines.

Under the traction loads on the cut surface of the test cell, the errors of NICE are at most 0.10% in the compliance and 0.16% in the sensitivity (Supplementary Table ST09).

An accurate compliance does not guarantee an accurate local sensitivity, so the two quantities have to be checked separately. For NICE, the sensitivity error exceeds the compliance error, but both lie far below the 3% line. Under the x traction load on the test-cell face of H1/x, its sensitivity error is about twice its compliance error (Supplementary Table ST25). Under the z traction load on the neighbour face of U1/x, where the test cell carries a small energy fraction, the two errors differ by four orders of magnitude. For the variants without the complete correction, the sensitivity error under this load exceeds the 3% line, while the compliance error stays far below it (Supplementary Table ST08 and Note S8).

The energy fraction of the test cell explains why the compliance error can be orders of magnitude smaller than the sensitivity error (Supplementary Table ST25). Consider the z traction load on the neighbour face of U1/x. Under this load, the test cell of NICE carries 0.127% of the exact assembled energy and has a local energy error of 0.0575%. Their product \(\beta=w\varepsilon\) bounds the relative compliance error by \(7.33\times10^{-5}\)%, close to the observed \(7.31\times10^{-5}\)%. The sensitivity error of the test cell is 0.598%, about 8,200 times the compliance error. Two factors make up this ratio. The compliance error is relative to the compliance of the whole assembly, so the local energy error enters it weighted by \(w\), which gives a factor \(1/w\approx780\). The sensitivity error is relative to the sensitivity of the test cell itself. This sensitivity scales with the energy of the test cell, as its error does, so that \(w\) cancels. At fixed retained displacements, the energy error controls the sensitivity error only at the order \(\sqrt\varepsilon\) (Proposition 4(b)). Here the sensitivity error is about ten times \(\varepsilon\). Figure 11 shows this weighting for NICE over all loads. The base network, Base network + correction and the two variants of Supplementary Note S8 follow the same relations (Figure S04).

### 5.7. Ablation: restricting the cell-face displacements

Keeping all retained DOFs matters more for the local sensitivity than for the compliance. To isolate this effect, pairs in configuration x are solved with the exact condensed stiffness of each cell, while the cell-face displacements of each cell are restricted to tensor-product Bernstein polynomials of degree \(r\). The DOFs of the cut-plane elements that do not lie on the cell faces remain unrestricted. The ablation restricts only the representation of the retained displacements of the present cells, at a fixed cell size. Learned substructures with a reduced boundary control the resulting error by refining the partition, enriching the boundary interpolation or oversampling [Huang et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jmps.2024.105893), [Guo et al. (2026a)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2026.118955), [Guo et al. (2026b)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22019v1). These remedies are not reproduced here (Supplementary Note S2). With every cell face restricted, degree one gives compliance errors of 78–85% on U1, M1, M2 and H1. The errors decrease to about 4% at \(r=5\) and to 0.49–0.74% at \(r=8\). At \(r=8\), the H1 pair has 14,001 retained DOFs instead of 32,991 (Figure 12).

The local sensitivity converges more slowly. At \(r=8\), the test-cell sensitivity error is 1.3–4.5% under the loads on the test-cell face and 24–64% when the loads on the neighbour face are included. Restricting only the shared interface removes most of the compliance error. At \(r=3\), the compliance error on U1, M1 and M2 is then 0.17–0.44%. Under the neighbour-face loads, however, the sensitivity error remains 8–21% at \(r=3\) and 2.2–3.4% at \(r=5\). A restricted interface is therefore adequate for the compliance at low degree, but not for the local sensitivity under neighbour-face loads. Keeping all retained DOFs removes this part of the error, at the cost of a larger assembled model (Supplementary Table ST11).
