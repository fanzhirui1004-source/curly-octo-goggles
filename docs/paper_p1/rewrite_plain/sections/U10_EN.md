### 5.8. Lattices of learned cells

In a design, every cell is a learned cell, that is, a cell represented by NICE. The errors of neighbouring cells then enter the same assembled solution. Two lattices with a continuously graded thickness field and a planar boundary cut test whether these errors accumulate. The first is a \(2\times2\times2\) block of eight distinct cells. Four of its cells are cut, with retained volume fractions of 62% and 25%. The second is a \(3\times3\times1\) layer of eight cells, three of which are cut, with one corner position left empty. Neighbouring cells share the corner parameters of their common faces. The corner parameters of the block range from 0.25 to 0.54. The sixteen cells were generated for these examples and were used neither in training nor in checkpoint selection. Each lattice is clamped on one face. It is loaded by unit traction loads on the opposite face in the three Cartesian directions, called the face loads, and by three random load vectors. For the sensitivities, the nodal load is held at its value for the evaluated design (Appendix H). The reference solution assembles the dense exact condensed stiffness matrices and is solved to a recomputed relative residual of at most \(1.3\times10^{-10}\). The lattices of learned cells are solved by preconditioned conjugate gradients on the free retained DOFs, with a balanced two-level preconditioner (Supplementary Note S4.1). Its fine-level action is the inverse of the assembled stiffness block of the retained DOFs, \(\mathbb K_{PP}=\sum_mB_m^TK_{PP,m}B_m\), which is factorised once per design iteration. Its coarse space consists of the trilinear lattice-vertex functions multiplied by the six rigid-body modes.

The errors of the cells do not accumulate in these lattices. Under the face loads, the compliance error stays below 0.015% in both lattices, and the sensitivity error of every cell is at most 0.144% (Table 3). The sensitivities of the cells, summed at the shared corner parameters, give the lattice gradient. Under the face loads, this gradient agrees with the exact one to within 0.09%, with cosines above 0.999999. Over all six loads, the assembled retained solutions differ from the exact ones by 0.13% and 0.12% in the relative Euclidean norm. These lattice errors are smaller than the largest errors of the two-cell assemblies of Section 5.6. The reason is Eq. (15), which bounds the compliance error of the lattice by the weighted energy error \(\beta\). In \(\beta\), the energy error of each cell at the exact retained displacements is weighted by the energy fraction of the cell. Under the face loads, these cell energy errors range from 0.006% to 0.026% in the block and from 0.005% to 0.052% in the layer. Under the random loads, they range from 0.02% to 0.12%. They are largest in the cut cells. Under the face loads, \(\beta\) exceeds the compliance error of the lattice by less than 0.4% of \(\beta\). Under the random loads, the excess is about 4–5%. The excess arises because the assembled solution adapts to the stiffer learned cells, whereas \(\beta\) evaluates the cell errors at the exact retained displacements.

The recomputed residual of these solves stagnates, but the errors above are errors of the condensed stiffness and not of the incomplete solve. The solves reach the prescribed tolerance on the recursively updated residual. The recomputed residual \(\|f_g-\widehat{\mathbb K}\bar U\|/\|f_g\|\), however, stagnates at \(3.6\times10^{-4}\) in the block and at \(3.2\times10^{-3}\) in the layer. This level is consistent with rounding in the single-precision network. It does not come from the correction: running the correction in single instead of double precision changes this level by less than 1% in the block. At the final iterate, the residual work \(\bar U^T\rho\) of Eq. (16) is at most \(2.5\times10^{-8}\) of the compliance. Supplementary Note S4.3 also records a dual-norm bound on the residual work and the action–energy inconsistency (Supplementary Table ST14). The latter is the difference between the energy computed with the applied condensed stiffness and the energy of the recovered displacements. Together with the residual work, these records show that the errors above do not come from the incomplete solve.

### 5.9. Computational cost

A lattice analysis with sensitivities by NICE needs less time and memory than the direct solution of the whole lattice and than conventional exact static condensation. It also needs less time than conjugate gradients on the whole lattice preconditioned by algebraic multigrid. The reason is that NICE solves the assembled problem on the retained DOFs only. For the two eight-cell lattices, these number 139,002 and 143,685, whereas the whole-lattice model has 1.83 and 2.11 million DOFs. NICE also prepares every cell by a network evaluation and a fixed correction instead of a factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix. At the scale of Section 5.10, the cut finite element model of a 110-cell plate has 32.7 million DOFs, and its assembled retained system has 1.6 million. A design iteration of this plate takes 19 min, about 11 s per cell. The cost comparison below concerns one lattice analysis with sensitivities, from cell setup to the solution. It uses the two eight-cell lattices of Section 5.8 and two four-cell lattices, which are the \(2\times2\times1\) layers of the block. Each layer has two uncut cells and two cut cells with retained volume fractions of 62% and 25%. Each layer keeps the retained DOFs, the clamp and the loads of the block. Table 5 compares three routes.

Route (a) is the direct solution of the whole lattice. It assembles the full cut finite element model of the lattice, which contains the retained and interior DOFs of every cell. This model is factorised once by the sparse Cholesky factorisation of MKL PARDISO, with 16 threads on one AMD EPYC 9654 CPU. Its recomputed relative residuals are below \(3\times10^{-9}\). For the four-cell lattices, route (a) therefore also serves as the exact reference.

Route (b) is conventional exact static condensation on the same CPU with 16 threads. Each cell is condensed by a Cholesky factorisation of its interior stiffness matrix with the Schur-complement option of PARDISO. The assembled condensed system is then solved by a block Cholesky factorisation. This factorisation eliminates the retained DOFs that belong to a single cell before the DOFs of the shared interface. The compliance of route (b) agrees with that of route (a) to within \(7\times10^{-10}\).

Route (c) is NICE on one GPU. It prepares the learned substructure of every cell and solves the assembled system by the preconditioned conjugate gradients of Section 5.8 to a relative residual of \(10^{-6}\). It then computes the sensitivities. In this route, the correction runs in single precision.

All routes include cell setup and stiffness assembly. None includes the one-off cost of data generation and training. The two exact routes solve six loads but do not evaluate sensitivities, which would add to their time. In route (a), the solution of the six loads takes 3–4% of the total time (Supplementary Table ST12b). The routes run on different processors, one GPU against 16 CPU threads. The comparison therefore concerns the routes as each is deployed and does not measure hardware-equivalent amounts of work.

For the two eight-cell lattices, an analysis with NICE takes 79 and 105 s. The whole-lattice direct solution takes 868 and 1,042 s, about ten times as long, and conventional exact static condensation takes longer still (Table 5). The four-cell lattices give similar ratios. On these lattices, the compliance computed with NICE agrees with that of the direct solution to within 0.022%. More threads help the direct solution little, because cell setup and assembly do not become faster. Supplementary Table ST12b gives the times with 32 threads and with one thread. In route (a), cell setup and stiffness assembly on the CPU take 41 to 53% of the time. In route (c), the cell preparation on the GPU contains setup and assembly as well as the network encoding and the setup of the correction. With these preparation phases excluded from both routes, NICE remains five to eight times faster (Supplementary Tables ST12b and ST12d). For the eight-cell lattices, the timed compliance errors include the algebraic error of the solve to \(10^{-6}\) (Eq. (16)). They exceed the errors of the condensed stiffness reported in Section 5.8 by up to 0.005 percentage points.

The memory requirements follow the same pattern. For the eight-cell lattices, the peak process memory of the direct solution is 71 and 86 GiB. This peak is set by the Cholesky factor, which grows slightly faster than the number of DOFs. Conventional exact static condensation needs 40 and 42 GiB, most of it for the condensed cell matrices, which are held until their elimination (Supplementary Table ST12). NICE needs at most 9.2 GiB of GPU memory and holds at most 4.7 GiB of CPU memory after cell preparation. Per cell, the stored state of NICE takes 0.25 to 1.37 GiB. In conventional exact static condensation, the interior Cholesky factor takes 0.60 to 9.4 GiB per cell (Supplementary Table ST13).

Iterative solvers for the whole lattice were compared on the two eight-cell lattices. They solve the systems of route (a) with PETSc [Balay et al. (2026)](https://petsc.org/release/manual/) on the same CPU with 16 MPI processes (Supplementary Table ST12e). Conjugate gradients preconditioned by smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511) reach a relative residual of \(10^{-4}\) in 52 to 84 s per load, after a setup of 35 and 39 s. At this residual, the compliance differs from that of the converged solution by less than \(5\times10^{-10}\). Including the setup and assembly of route (a), the analysis of the three loads of route (c) takes 637 and 752 s, eight and seven times as long as NICE. Classical algebraic multigrid (BoomerAMG [Falgout & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-47789-6_66)) gave an indefinite preconditioner for one lattice and needed 250 to 260 iterations per load for the other. For BDDC [Dohrmann (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1137/S1064827502412887) with exact local solvers and one subdomain per cell, the factorisation of the Dirichlet and complete Neumann matrices of every cell alone takes 449 and 523 s (Supplementary Note S3). Every fine-scale route repeats the cell setup and the assembly of the whole-lattice system. In route (a), these two steps take 416 and 474 s (Supplementary Table ST12b), which alone is 5.3 and 4.5 times the time of the NICE analysis.

In a design iteration, the geometry of every cell changes. The preparation of every cell is therefore repeated, as in the analyses of Table 5. For the conventional routes, this preparation is the factorisation of the interior stiffness matrix; for NICE, it is the network encoding and the setup of the correction. The assembled solve can start from the previous solution. The conventional routes can reuse their symbolic factorisation as long as the pattern of active elements does not change.

### 5.10. Thickness design

Two cases are optimised with NICE. Case A is the \(2\times2\times2\) block of Section 5.8. The second case is a cut plate supported on its cut, which is described below and shown in Figure 13. In both cases, the corner thickness parameters solve the design problem of Section 2.3 under one unit traction load on a face, with \(V^*=0.8\,V(\boldsymbol\tau^0)\). The parameters at the vertices in the plane of the loaded face are fixed; these are 9 of 27 parameters in case A and 10 of 74 in the plate. This keeps the nodal load independent of the design and removes the load-derivative term \(2f_{g,c}^T\widehat U\) of Appendix H. The optimiser receives the vertex gradient of Section 2.3, with the sensitivities \(\widetilde{\boldsymbol s}_m\) of Section 4.2 in place of \(\boldsymbol s_m\). The bounds are \(0.18\le\tau\le0.69\). The corner span of a cell is the largest difference between two of its corner parameters. It is limited to 0.45 in each cell, and so is the norm of the gradient of \(\tau\). These bounds and limits keep every cell inside the parameter domain of the training data. In this domain, the corner parameters lie in [0.175, 0.699], and the corner span and the gradient norm are at most 0.47 (Section 6.3). Each design iteration regenerates the geometry and the learned substructure of every cell. It then solves the lattice from the previous solution by the preconditioned conjugate gradients of Section 5.8. Finally, it takes one step of the method of moving asymptotes (MMA) [Svanberg (1987)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1620240207), in the variant of Svanberg (2007). The move limit is 5% of the parameter range, and no line search is used (Supplementary Table ST16).

In case A, the NICE design agrees with the design obtained with exact static condensation. In this case, the block is loaded by a normal traction on the face opposite the clamp. It is optimised from its graded design with NICE and, for comparison, with exact static condensation; the latter is called the twin run (Figure 14a). NICE stops after 24 design iterations and exact static condensation after 23 (Table 6). Both runs stop when the relative change of the objective has stayed below \(10^{-4}\) in three consecutive iterations (Supplementary Table ST16). No Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) residual is used for stopping, because the discrete model, and with it the objective, changes between design iterations (Section 4.2). Supplementary Note S6.1 explains this choice. Both runs lower the compliance by 2.0% with 20% less material. Their final corner parameters differ by at most 0.0051. The exact compliances of their final designs differ by \(1.25\times10^{-5}\) of their value. At the three checked designs, the NICE compliance lies at most 0.028% below the exact one, with the sign given by Eq. (15). The lattice gradient has errors of at most 0.33%, and every component has the exact sign (Table 3). Both errors grow as the design approaches the bounds (Supplementary Table ST17).

The plate supported on its cut is a single layer of \(8\times4\) cells. A planar cut removes eight of these cells and leaves 16 uncut and eight cut cells (Supplementary Note S6.3). The plate is clamped at its cut: every DOF of the cut-plane elements of every cut cell is fixed. This support lies within one element, of size \(h=1/32\), of the cut surface. Because the DOFs of the cut-plane elements are retained, NICE represents this support without retraining. The end face of the plate is four cells wide. It carries a traction load of unit resultant that acts in the plane of the plate and normal to its long side. The plate thus acts as an in-plane cantilever supported along the oblique cut (Figure 15a). The cut normal, with \(\vartheta=33.7^\circ\), lies in the range \(0<\vartheta<\pi/4\) of orientations in which NICE was mainly evaluated (Sections 5.1 and 6.3).

Starting from the uniform design \(\tau=0.40\), NICE stops after 24 design iterations by the rule on the change of the objective (Figure 14b). The compliance rises from 75.59 to 110.49 while the volume is reduced to \(V^*\), and then falls to 78.26. The final design reaches both bounds as well as the corner-span and gradient limits. Its thickest walls lie near the end of the support closest to the loaded face and along the opposite free edge. The cells far from the load are thinned to the lower bound (Figure 15a). Checked with exact static condensation, the NICE compliance lies 0.011% below the exact one at the uniform start and 0.030% below it at the final design. At these two designs, the lattice gradient has errors of 0.029% and 0.30%, and all 64 components have the exact sign (Supplementary Table ST21). Two uniform designs of the same volume serve as references. In the first, the ten parameters of the loaded face are kept at 0.40, and the other 64 take the common value that meets \(V^*\). In the second, all 74 parameters take the same value. Against the first uniform design, the NICE design lowers the exact compliance by 32.1%, from 115.36 to 78.29. Against the second, it lowers the exact compliance by 30.9%. The homogenisation design described below lowers it by 30.5% against the first. For both uniform designs, the NICE compliance agrees with the exact one to within 0.019%, and the gradient to within 0.064% (Supplementary Table ST21).

For comparison, the plate is also optimised with a homogenised model, as in homogenisation-based graded design [Li et al. (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cad.2018.06.003). The elasticity tensor of this model is that of the cell with uniform thickness, obtained by periodic homogenisation on the same discrete model (Supplementary Table ST18b). The homogenised model is clamped on the plane of the cut. It is optimised with the same variables, bounds, limits, volume bound and optimiser as the NICE run. Its volume is computed from the homogenised material volume fraction \(V^H(\tau)\). At the uniform start, this volume equals the material volume of the discrete model to within \(1.2\times10^{-7}\). The homogenised model underestimates the exact compliance of the uniform start by 27.0%. This is consistent with the absence of scale separation through the thickness of a single layer. With 12 instead of 6 elements per cell and axis, the underestimate is 25.8%. A hundredfold larger penalty factor changes the macroscale compliance by less than 0.001% (Supplementary Note S6.3). The underestimate is not caused by the support. The fine-scale model clamps all cut-plane elements, a support at least as stiff as one acting on the cut surface alone. This support lowers the exact compliance and therefore narrows the difference. Along its own optimisation path, the macroscale compliance lies 27–33% below the NICE compliance; up to iteration 3, the two runs analyse the same designs (Figure 14b). Analysed on the cut geometry, the homogenisation design uses 0.18% less material than the NICE design, and its compliance is 2.44% higher. This difference is the same whether both designs are evaluated with NICE or with exact static condensation, which gives exact compliances of 80.198 and 78.287 (Supplementary Tables ST18c and ST21). The corner parameters of the homogenisation design differ from those of the NICE design by up to 0.26, with a correlation of 0.95 (Supplementary Table ST18c). Figure 15b shows these corner parameters. The homogenised model thus underestimates the compliance of the cut layer by about a quarter on both macroscale meshes. It also leads to a design that is less stiff on the cut geometry.

The discrete model of a cell can change from one design to the next (Section 4.2). In both cases, the discrete model changed between every pair of consecutive design iterations. The change affected at least 3 of the 8 cells of case A and at least 11 of the 24 cells of the plate (Supplementary Table ST19b). In these runs, the residual work of Eq. (16) stayed at most \(5.4\times10^{-8}\) of the compliance. In four design iterations over all runs, the geometry generator required a perturbation of at most \(5.8\times10^{-4}\) in a corner parameter (Supplementary Table ST19a). A NICE design iteration took 74 s on average for case A and 200 s for the plate (Table 6). For the plate, single iterations took 155–270 s, and the 24 iterations took 1.3 h in total (Supplementary Table ST18a).

To examine how the cost grows with the size of the lattice, the design iteration was timed on plates with 24, 51, 88 and 110 cells. These plates have the proportions and the cut of the plate above. They are clamped along the uncut long side and loaded in plane on the opposite face (Figure 16). The largest plate has 32.7 million DOFs in the cut finite element model and 1.62 million free retained DOFs. Each plate was run for its first four design iterations. Learned substructures beyond 4 GiB of GPU memory were streamed from CPU memory. The time of a design iteration grew in proportion to the number of cells, at 10.1 to 10.9 s per cell, and reached 19.4 min for 110 cells. The conjugate gradients needed 138 to 173 iterations. The CPU memory, which holds the streamed state of the learned cells, grew by about 0.7 GiB per cell. With the learned cells on the GPU held within a fixed 4 GiB, the GPU memory grew with the assembled retained system. The preconditioner factorises the stiffness matrix \(\mathbb K_{PP}\) of this system directly (Supplementary Table ST20). Exact checks of the 24- and 51-cell plates at their first design iteration give the compliance and gradient errors listed in Table 3. Every gradient component has the exact sign.
