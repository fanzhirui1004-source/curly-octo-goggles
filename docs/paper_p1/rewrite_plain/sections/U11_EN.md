## 6. Discussion

### 6.1. Retained degrees of freedom, network and correction

Using the complete set of retained DOFs, without reducing the interface, separates two choices that a reduced substructure model otherwise combines. The first is which displacement patterns neighbouring cells can exchange, and the second is how accurately the interior responds to each of them. The network and the correction affect only the second choice. The ablation of Section 5.7 shows the role of the first choice. Even with exact interior displacements, restricting the cell-face displacements changes the assembled response. A polynomial degree that gives an accurate compliance can still leave an appreciable error in the sensitivity. In the two-cell assemblies of the cut cells examined, restricting the cell-face displacements produced larger errors in the local sensitivity than the interior approximation of NICE (Sections 5.6 and 5.7). This comparison was made at a fixed cell size and without the refinement of the partition or the enrichment that methods with reduced boundary DOFs use. The retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements carry the support of the plate of Section 5.10 directly. The homogenised model needs a separate constraint on the plane of the cut for this support. Where the cut surfaces carry no loads or supports, these DOFs enlarge the assembled system (Section 6.3). The solution of the assembled system takes most of the time of a NICE analysis (Supplementary Table ST12d).

The structure of the network makes it possible to learn the displacement recovery on the complete set of retained DOFs (Section 3.2). Its weights act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels, so one network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves every set of retained DOFs. The same network, unchanged, was used for the 80 validation geometries, the sixteen cells of the lattices and the cut cells of the plate supported on its cut (Sections 5.3, 5.8 and 5.10). Because the network output is exactly linear in the retained displacements, the relations of Sections 3.1 and 4 apply to the network itself (Section 3.2). Because the correction is linear and fixed for each geometry, the network can be trained with the correction applied (Section 3.5). The condensed stiffness is applied without being formed. Each cell therefore stores the quantities determined by its geometry rather than a dense matrix on its retained DOFs (Section 3.6).

Within the interior, the network prediction and the correction are complementary. The energy formulation of the condensed stiffness shows the role of each. Consider an interior correction that does not increase the \(A\)-energy error for any retained displacements. Whatever the network, the corrected condensed stiffness then lies between the uncorrected condensed stiffness and the exact condensed stiffness (Section 3.4). An interior displacement error of about one percent gives an energy error of 1.8 to 35%, because the error is stiffer than the exact solution (Section 5.4). Chebyshev smoothing removes the error components with large Rayleigh quotients of the Jacobi-scaled interior stiffness matrix. The coarse-grid correction removes the smooth components that smoothing reduces slowly. Under the same correction, the network prediction leaves lower energy errors than zero interior displacements and the graph-harmonic field (Table 4). Even with eight times the smoothing work, the graph-harmonic field leaves a larger error than the eight-step result of the base network in five of six cells (Section 5.5). Conversely, the correction reduces the error components that the network represents poorly. On the moderately cut cell M1, it lowers the energy error of the base network from 13.5% to 0.19% without any change of the network parameters. In the two-cell configurations, Base network + correction already stays below the 3% lines. Training with the correction applied adds less than the correction itself. It reduces the mean energy error by a further factor of 1.31 (Section 5.3).

### 6.2. Computational value

A number of correction steps that suffices for the compliance can leave the local sensitivity inaccurate. The relevant cost is therefore the total work needed to reach the prescribed accuracy in both quantities. On the lattices of Section 5.9, NICE is faster than the whole-lattice direct solution and conventional exact static condensation. On the two eight-cell lattices, on which iterative solvers were compared, it is also faster than whole-lattice conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid. It also needs less memory than the two direct routes. The advantage comes from the way the interior is resolved. The exact routes, direct or iterative, solve the fine-scale problem of every cell or of the whole lattice. NICE obtains the interior displacements of each cell from one network evaluation and a fixed number of correction steps, and it solves the assembled problem on the retained DOFs only (Section 5.9). Inexact BDDC and FETI-DP methods [Li & Widlund (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2006.03.011), [Klawonn & Rheinbach (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1758), [Hirschler et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.7419) use approximate subdomain and coarse solves only inside the preconditioner, and therefore converge to the exact discrete solution. The lattice solve of NICE, in contrast, iterates on the assembled condensed stiffness matrices \(\widehat S\) of the learned substructures, so the approximation lies in the operator itself. Fewer smoothing steps would lower the cost of applying the condensed stiffness, at the loss of accuracy quantified in Section 5.5.

### 6.3. Limitations

The trained network applies to the setting in which it was trained and tested. This setting consists of Schwarz-P-type cells of Eq. (1) with eight corner parameters in [0.175, 0.699] and with corner span and gradient norm of at most 0.47 (Supplementary Table ST02). Each cell has at most one planar cut, whose normal is the image of \((\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta,0)\) under a symmetry of the cube. The network is also tied to one discretisation, with \(n=32\), \(E_Y=1\), \(\nu=0.3\) and \(\gamma=10^{-4}\). Its grid hierarchy, with 65, 33, 17 and 9 positions per axis, is matched to this discretisation. Other TPMS families need further training. On gyroid sheet cells, the network trained on Schwarz-P cells gives mean energy errors under loads and supports that are 56 to 234 times those on the corresponding Schwarz-P cells. A further 8,000 training steps with eighteen gyroid cells lower these errors six- to tenfold, but they remain 6 to 23 times the Schwarz-P level (Supplementary Note S7). Other families therefore require new training data and network parameters, as do cells with several cuts or curved boundaries, other resolutions and other materials. Training on several families and testing on a family held out would show how far the network itself transfers; this is left to future work. The network is not equivariant under the symmetries of the cube: for a rotated or reflected cell, its prediction is in general not the correspondingly rotated or reflected prediction. Symmetry-decoupled shape functions for voxel substructures have this property [Jiang, C. et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compstruct.2026.120865). NICE was evaluated mainly in the identity orientation. It was also evaluated on the 80 validation geometries in a second orientation, obtained by a symmetry of the cube. This orientation was also used in checkpoint selection. In this orientation, its mean energy error over the seven load classes evaluated in both orientations is 4% higher in relative terms. Under traction loads, it is 12% higher (Supplementary Table ST03c).

The properties of the condensed stiffness shown in Section 3.1 hold for any input to the network, but the accuracy does not. No certified error bound is provided (Appendix G.4). Because \(\widehat S\succeq S\), the learned model is stiffer than the reference model and underestimates the compliance (Eq. (15)). Its compliance is therefore not a conservative estimate. The interior residual of Eq. (7) is available at run time. The energy error that it determines, however, requires one interior solve per test displacement. The lower bound of Appendix I needs no reference solution. It can detect a large error for a test displacement but cannot confirm a small one. Accuracy is measured against the discrete reference model (Section 5.2). At \(n=32\), this model deviates from its refinement to \(n=64\) by at most 0.28% in the compliance and 0.44% in the sensitivity. The exception is the heavily cut cell H2, where the deviations reach about 1% and 3.6% (Supplementary Table ST10).

The evidence on assembled models consists of the following examples. The two-cell assemblies comprise fourteen configurations of cells used for checkpoint selection and eighteen configurations of cells outside checkpoint selection (Section 5.6). The lattices are two eight-cell lattices and the two four-cell layers of the block (Sections 5.8 and 5.9). The designs checked against exact static condensation are three designs of the block in case A and five designs of the cut 24-cell plate. They also include the first designs of the 24- and 51-cell plates of the scale study (Section 5.10). All comparisons between trained variants use a single random seed. The effect of the sensitivity term in the loss of Eq. (13) was not isolated. The reported sensitivities are \(\widetilde s_c=-\widehat u^TK_{,c}\widehat u\). They omit the design dependence of the recovery operator \(F\), which enters the derivative of Eq. (18). A central-difference check of the approximate compliance cannot decide whether \(\widehat C_{,c}\) or \(\widetilde s_c\) is the more accurate gradient. At least one of the discrete choices listed in Appendix B.1 switches in 21 to 48 of the 49 rebuilds of each lattice cell. In addition, the difference quotient grows as the step decreases (Supplementary Note S4.3). The sensitivity \(\widetilde s_c\) needs no difference quotient. For the two eight-cell lattices and the two four-cell layers, it gives the gradient with respect to the shared parameters to within 0.02–0.13% under the face loads. Under the random loads, the gradient is within 0.22–0.35% (Supplementary Table ST14). The optimisations of Section 5.10 take steps without a line search. A line search would compare the approximate compliance with \(\widetilde s_c\), which is not its derivative. Where the cut surfaces carry no loads or supports, the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements enlarge the assembled system. In the lattices of Sections 5.8 and 5.9, the DOFs of the cut-plane elements that do not lie on the cell faces make up 37 to 50% of the free retained DOFs. A reduced representation of these DOFs where the cut carries no loads or supports is a natural next step. On one GPU, the size of the lattice that can be analysed is at present limited by the direct factorisation of \(\mathbb K_{PP}\) in the preconditioner. The memory of this factorisation grows with the assembled retained system, whereas the memory of the learned cells stays within a fixed budget. A further level in the preconditioner or an iterative coarse solve is the natural way to remove this limit.

Fine-scale iterative solvers of the whole lattice were compared on the two eight-cell lattices only. The comparison used algebraic multigrid [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511), [Henson & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0168-9274(01)00115-5), [Falgout & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-47789-6_66) in standard settings, and the local factorisation cost of BDDC with exact local solvers [Dohrmann (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1137/S1064827502412887) (Section 5.9). FETI-DP [Farhat et al. (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.76) and tuned multigrid were not compared. An optimiser must keep the designs inside the parameter domain stated above, as the bounds and limits of Section 5.10 do. The optimisations of that section are local.

## 7. Conclusions

NICE is a learned component model that keeps the properties of static condensation on the complete set of retained DOFs of a cut cell. This set changes with the cell geometry. A network predicts the interior displacements, and a fixed two-grid correction reduces the interior force imbalance that the network leaves. The condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy of the corrected field and applied with the transpose of the complete recovery operator. It is therefore symmetric positive semidefinite, and only the rigid-body modes have zero energy. It is never softer than the exact condensed stiffness, and its error is quadratic in the interior displacement error (Proposition 1). These properties do not depend on how well the network is trained. A correction that satisfies the conditions of Proposition 2 cannot increase the error of the condensed stiffness. In every example of Supplementary Table ST07, a larger number of smoothing steps reduced the error further. The network and the correction complement each other. Under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field leaves 5.4 to 265 times the error of NICE. Applied to the base network without retraining, the correction alone lowers the mean energy error under traction loads on the 80 validation geometries from 6.89% to 0.0965%.

The error relations of Section 4 determine the quantities on which such a model must be checked. The interior error enters the assembled compliance weighted by the energy fraction of each cell (Proposition 3). It enters the thickness sensitivity through a term linear in that error (Proposition 4). In cut cells, the displacement errors of the base network are about one percent, but they are stiffer than the exact solution and give energy errors of 1.8 to 35% (Section 5.4). These relations hold for any approximate recovery operator. An approximate static condensation used in design must therefore be checked on its sensitivities as well as on its compliance. NICE is trained and checked on both.

For cut thin-walled Schwarz-P cells with tens of thousands of retained DOFs, the mean energy error of NICE over the 80 validation geometries is 0.074%. In every two-cell configuration, its compliance and sensitivity errors are at most 0.27% and 1.49%. An analysis of an eight-cell lattice with sensitivities takes 79–105 s on one GPU. On 16 CPU threads or processes, the whole-lattice direct solution takes 868–1,042 s, and whole-lattice conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid take 637–752 s. Thickness optimisation with NICE reproduces the design that exact static condensation gives for an eight-cell lattice, to within 0.0051 in every corner parameter. For a 24-cell plate clamped at its cut, a homogenised model underestimates the compliance by 27%. For the same plate, the compliance and the gradient from NICE differ from those of exact static condensation by at most 0.032% and 0.32% at the five checked designs. The optimised design has a 32% lower compliance than the uniform design of the same volume. On the plates of the scale study, exact checks up to 51 cells give compliance errors below 0.014% and gradient errors below 0.05%. A design iteration of a 110-cell plate with 32.7 million DOFs takes 19 min.

Homogenisation loses accuracy where the scales do not separate, and a fine-scale model of every wall must be solved again at every design iteration. Between these two approaches, NICE provides a component model that keeps the properties of static condensation without factorising the interior stiffness matrix of each cell. Including the coarse-grid correction in the network, as a coarse level that solves the coarse-grid problem with the matrix \(V^TAV\), would leave the smoothing as the only fixed stage. The properties of the condensed stiffness follow from its computation through the strain energy and do not depend on the network. The error relations and the correction therefore apply to any family of components with a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix and a positive definite interior block. For a new family of cells, the network has to be retrained, whereas the error relations and the correction carry over unchanged.
