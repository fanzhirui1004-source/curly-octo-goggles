## 6. Discussion

### 6.1. Retained degrees of freedom, network and correction

Using the complete set of retained DOFs, without reducing the interface, separates two choices that a reduced substructure model combines: the displacement patterns that neighbouring cells can exchange, and the accuracy with which the interior responds to each of them. The network and the correction affect only the interior response, whereas the ablation of Section 5.7 isolates the effect of the exchanged displacement patterns. Even with exact interior displacements, restricting the cell-face displacements changes the assembled response, and a polynomial degree that gives an accurate compliance can still leave an appreciable error in the local sensitivity. In the two-cell assemblies of the cut cells examined, restricting the cell-face displacements produced larger errors in the local sensitivity than the interior approximation of NICE (Sections 5.6 and 5.7). This comparison was made at a fixed cell size and without the refinement of the partition or the enrichment that methods with reduced boundary DOFs use. In addition, the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements carry the support of the plate of Section 5.10 directly, whereas the homogenised model needs a separate constraint on the plane of the cut for this support. Where the cut surfaces carry no loads or supports, however, these DOFs enlarge the assembled system (Section 6.3).

The structure of the network makes it possible to learn the displacement recovery on the complete set of retained DOFs (Section 3.2). Its weights act on local node positions, feature channels and grid levels, so one network of about \(6\times10^5\) parameters serves every set of retained DOFs. The same network, unchanged, was used for the 80 validation geometries, the sixteen cells of the lattices and the cut cells of the plate supported on its cut (Sections 5.3, 5.8 and 5.10). Because the network output is exactly linear in the retained displacements, the relations of Sections 3.1 and 4 apply to the network itself (Section 3.2), and because the correction is linear and fixed for each geometry, the network can be trained with the correction applied (Section 3.5). The condensed stiffness is applied without being formed, so each cell stores the quantities determined by its geometry rather than a dense matrix on its retained DOFs (Section 3.6).

Within the interior, the network prediction and the correction are complementary. If a correction does not increase the \(A\)-energy error for any retained displacements, the corrected condensed stiffness lies between the condensed stiffness before the correction and the exact one, whatever the network (Section 3.4). The error left by the network is stiffer than the exact solution, so an interior displacement error of about 1% gives an energy error of 1.8 to 35% (Section 5.4). Chebyshev smoothing removes the components of this error with large Rayleigh quotients of the Jacobi-scaled interior stiffness matrix, and the coarse-grid correction removes the smooth components that smoothing reduces slowly. Under the same correction, the network prediction leaves lower errors than zero interior displacements and the graph-harmonic field, and in five of six cells this remains so with eight times the smoothing work (Section 5.5). Most of the reduction of the error comes from the correction: applied at deployment alone, it lowers the mean energy error of the base network by about two orders of magnitude, whereas NICE, trained further with the correction applied and on geometries drawn from a larger set, lowers it by a further factor of 1.31 (Section 5.3).

### 6.2. Computational value

A number of correction steps that suffices for the compliance can leave the local sensitivity inaccurate, so the relevant cost is the total work needed to reach the prescribed accuracy in both quantities. On the lattices of Section 5.9, NICE is faster than the whole-lattice direct solution and conventional exact static condensation and needs less memory than these two direct routes; on the two eight-cell lattices on which iterative solvers were compared, it is also faster than whole-lattice conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid. The advantage comes from the way the interior is resolved. The exact routes, direct or iterative, solve the fine-scale problem of every cell or of the whole lattice, whereas NICE obtains the interior displacements of each cell at given retained displacements from one network evaluation and a fixed number of correction steps, and solves the assembled problem on the retained DOFs only (Section 5.9). The work thus moves from the factorisation of each cell interior to the assembled solve: every iteration applies the condensed stiffness (the network, the correction and their transposes) and the preconditioner, which takes most of the time of a NICE analysis (Supplementary Table ST12d), and the size of the lattice that can be analysed is limited by the direct factorisation of \(\mathbb K_{PP}\) in the preconditioner (Section 6.3). Inexact BDDC and FETI-DP methods [Li & Widlund (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cma.2006.03.011), [Klawonn & Rheinbach (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.1758), [Hirschler et al. (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.7419) use approximate subdomain and coarse solves only inside the preconditioner and therefore converge to the exact discrete solution. The lattice solve of NICE, in contrast, iterates on the assembled condensed stiffness matrices \(\widehat S\) of the learned substructures, so the approximation lies in the operator itself. Fewer smoothing steps would lower the cost of applying the condensed stiffness, at the loss of accuracy quantified in Section 5.5.

### 6.3. Limitations

The trained network applies to the setting in which it was trained and tested: Schwarz-P-type cells of Eq. (1) with eight corner parameters in [0.175, 0.699] and with corner span and gradient norm of at most 0.47 (Supplementary Table ST02). Each cell has at most one planar cut, whose normal is the image of \((\cos\vartheta,\sin\vartheta,0)\) under a symmetry of the cube. The network is also tied to one discretisation, with \(n=32\), \(E_Y=1\), \(\nu=0.3\) and \(\gamma=10^{-4}\), to which its grid hierarchy, with 65, 33, 17 and 9 positions per axis, is matched. On gyroid sheet cells, the network trained on Schwarz-P cells gives mean energy errors under loads and supports that are 56 to 234 times those on the corresponding Schwarz-P cells. A further 8,000 training steps with eighteen gyroid cells lower these errors six- to tenfold, but they remain 10 to 23 times the Schwarz-P level (Supplementary Note S7). Other families therefore require new training data and network parameters, as do cells with several cuts or curved boundaries, other resolutions and other materials; training on several families and testing on a family held out would show how far the network itself transfers, which is left to future work. The network is not equivariant under the symmetries of the cube: for a rotated or reflected cell, its prediction is in general not the correspondingly rotated or reflected prediction, a property that symmetry-decoupled shape functions for voxel substructures have [Jiang, C. et al. (2026)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compstruct.2026.120865). NICE was evaluated mainly in the identity orientation. In a second orientation, obtained by a symmetry of the cube and also used in checkpoint selection, its mean energy error on the 80 validation geometries over the seven load classes evaluated in both orientations is 4% higher in relative terms, and under traction loads 12% higher (Supplementary Table ST03c).

The properties of the condensed stiffness shown in Section 3.1 hold for any network parameters, but the accuracy does not, and no certified error bound is provided (Appendix G.4). Because \(\widehat S\succeq S\), the learned model is stiffer than the reference model and underestimates the compliance (Eq. (15)), so its compliance is not a conservative estimate. The interior residual of Eq. (7) is available at run time, but the energy error that it determines requires one interior solve per test displacement. The lower bound of Appendix I needs no reference solution and can detect a large error for a test displacement, but it cannot confirm a small one. Accuracy is measured against the discrete reference model (Section 5.2). At \(n=32\), this model deviates from its refinement by at most 0.28% in the compliance and 0.44% in the sensitivity, except in the heavily cut cell H2, where the deviations reach about 1% and 3.6% (Supplementary Table ST10).

Exact checks of assembled models and designs are limited to the examples of Tables 3 and 5: 32 two-cell configurations, two eight-cell lattices, the two four-cell layers of the block, and some of the designs of the two optimisation cases and of the scale study. All comparisons between trained variants use a single random seed, and the effect of the sensitivity term in the loss of Eq. (13) was not isolated.

The reported sensitivities are \(\widetilde s_c=-\widehat u^TK_{,c}\widehat u\); they omit the design dependence of the recovery operator \(\widehat\Psi\), which enters the derivative of Eq. (18). A central-difference check of the approximate compliance cannot decide whether \(\widehat C_{,c}\) or \(\widetilde s_c\) is the more accurate gradient. At least one of the discrete choices listed in Appendix B.1 switches in 21 to 48 of the 49 rebuilds of each lattice cell, and the difference quotient grows as the step decreases (Supplementary Note S4.3). The sensitivity \(\widetilde s_c\) needs no difference quotient; for the two eight-cell lattices and the two four-cell layers, it gives the gradient with respect to the shared parameters to within 0.02–0.13% under the face loads and 0.22–0.35% under the random loads (Supplementary Table ST14). The optimisations of Section 5.10 take steps without a line search, because a line search would compare the approximate compliance with \(\widetilde s_c\), which is not its derivative.

Where the cut surfaces carry no loads or supports, the retained DOFs of the cut-plane elements enlarge the assembled system. In the lattices of Sections 5.8 and 5.9, the DOFs of the cut-plane elements that do not lie on the cell faces make up 37 to 50% of the free retained DOFs. A reduced representation of these DOFs where the cut carries no loads or supports is a natural next step. On one GPU, the size of the lattice that can be analysed is at present limited by the direct factorisation of \(\mathbb K_{PP}\) in the preconditioner. The memory of this factorisation grows with the assembled retained system, whereas the memory of the learned cells stays within a fixed budget; a further level in the preconditioner or an iterative coarse solve is the natural way to remove this limit.

Fine-scale iterative solvers of the whole lattice were compared on the two eight-cell lattices only, using algebraic multigrid [Vaněk et al. (1996)](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF02238511), [Henson & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0168-9274(01)00115-5), [Falgout & Yang (2002)](https://doi.org/10.1007/3-540-47789-6_66) in standard settings and the local factorisation cost of BDDC with exact local solvers [Dohrmann (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1137/S1064827502412887) (Section 5.9). FETI-DP [Farhat et al. (2001)](https://doi.org/10.1002/nme.76) and tuned multigrid were not compared. An optimiser must keep the designs inside the parameter domain stated above, as the bounds and limits of Section 5.10 do, and the optimisations of that section are local.

## 7. Conclusions

NICE is a learned component model that keeps the properties of static condensation on the complete set of retained DOFs of a cut cell, a set that changes with the cell geometry. A network predicts the interior displacements, a fixed two-grid correction reduces the interior force imbalance that the network leaves, and the condensed stiffness is computed from the strain energy of the corrected field and applied with the transpose of the complete recovery operator. The condensed stiffness is therefore symmetric positive semidefinite, only the rigid-body modes have zero energy, and it is an upper bound on the exact condensed stiffness, with an error of second order in the interior displacement error (Proposition 1). These properties do not depend on how well the network is trained. A correction that satisfies the conditions of Proposition 2 cannot increase the error of the condensed stiffness, and in every example of Supplementary Table ST07, a larger number of smoothing steps reduced the error further. The network and the correction complement each other. Under the same correction, a graph-harmonic initial field leaves 5.4 to 265 times the error of NICE (Table 4); applied to the base network without retraining, the correction alone lowers the mean energy error under traction loads on the 80 validation geometries from 6.89% to 0.0965%.

The error relations of Section 4 determine the quantities on which such a model must be checked: the interior error enters the assembled compliance weighted by the energy fraction of each cell (Proposition 3) and the thickness sensitivity through a term of first order in that error (Proposition 4). In cut cells, the displacement errors of the base network are about 1%, but they are stiffer than the exact solution and give energy errors of 1.8 to 35% (Section 5.4). These relations hold for any approximate recovery operator that is linear and reproduces the retained displacements and rigid-body motions, so an approximate static condensation used in design must be checked on its sensitivities as well as on its compliance; NICE is trained on the energy and the sensitivities and checked on the compliance and the sensitivities.

For thin-walled Schwarz-P cells with tens of thousands of retained DOFs, the mean energy error of NICE under traction loads on the 80 validation geometries is 0.074%, and in every two-cell configuration its compliance and sensitivity errors are at most 0.27% and 1.49%. Two eight-cell lattices have compliance errors below 0.015% and gradient errors within 0.09% under face loads. An analysis of an eight-cell lattice with sensitivities takes 79–105 s on one GPU, whereas on 16 CPU threads or processes the whole-lattice direct solution takes 868–1,042 s and whole-lattice conjugate gradients with algebraic multigrid take 637–752 s. Thickness optimisation with NICE reproduces the design that exact static condensation gives for an eight-cell lattice, to within 0.0051 in every corner parameter. For a 24-cell plate clamped at its cut, a homogenised model underestimates the compliance of the uniform starting design by 27%. At the five checked designs of this plate, the compliance and the gradient from NICE differ from those of exact static condensation by at most 0.032% and 0.32%, and the optimised design has a 32% lower compliance than the uniform design of the same volume; the homogenisation design uses 0.18% less material than the NICE design and has a 2.44% higher compliance. On the plates of the scale study, exact checks up to 51 cells give compliance errors below 0.014% and gradient errors below 0.05%, and a design iteration of a 110-cell plate with 32.7 million DOFs takes 19 min.

The periodic boundary conditions assumed by homogenisation fail without scale separation or when cells are cut, and a fine-scale model that discretises every cell must be reassembled and solved at every design iteration. Between these two approaches, NICE provides a component model that keeps the properties of static condensation without factorising the interior stiffness matrix of each cell. The properties of the condensed stiffness follow from its construction and hold for any network parameters. The error relations and the correction therefore apply to any family of components with a symmetric positive semidefinite stiffness matrix and a positive definite interior block; for a new family of cells, the network has to be retrained, whereas the error relations and the correction carry over unchanged.
